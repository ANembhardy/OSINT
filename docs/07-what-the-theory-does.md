# What the theory is actually for

Plain-language companion to `docs/05` and `docs/06`. Read this one first. No
citation apparatus — the point here is the mechanics, not the reading list.

---

## 1. Why "what is my theory?" has felt unanswerable

Because the project needs **three** theoretical components doing three different
jobs, and the question "what is my theory" assumes there is one. There isn't, and
that is not a defect in the project. No single IR theory covers all three.

| | The question it answers | What goes here | How big |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Slot 1** | Why does Russia want to contest an order it cannot overturn by force? | Realism / neoclassical realism | One section. Background. |
| **Slot 2** | Why is *law* one of the tools it reaches for — how can legal argument be a resource of power at all? | IR–international law theory: Hurd, Koskenniemi, Simpson, Goddard | **Where the thesis actually lives.** |
| **Slot 3** | Is Russia's use of that tool directed, routine, habitual, or inherited? | Four rival accounts (§4) | Your findings chapter. |

Trying to make realism answer all three is what produces the stuck feeling. Realism
answers slot 1 well, slot 2 thinly, and slot 3 not at all.

## 2. What a theory does, in one sentence

> **A theory converts something you cannot observe into something you can.**

You cannot observe whether Russia "has a lawfare strategy." Strategy is a state of
mind and an internal process; the archives are shut. What you *can* observe is legal
acts: declarations filed, arguments made, objections raised, treaties exited,
statutes passed.

Theory is the bridge between the two. It says: *if X were true, the world would also
look like this.* That is what turns a pile of observed acts into evidence about an
unobservable. Without it you have a chronology. With it you have an argument.

This is the whole answer to "what is the theory for my piece of work". It is not a
hat you put on the thesis. It is the thing that makes your observations mean
something.

## 3. The distinction nobody explains

There are two different things called "theory" in a thesis, and confusing them is
the most common source of exactly the trouble you are having.

- **Theory as a lens.** A set of concepts you look through. "I approach this from a
  realist perspective." It cannot be wrong, because it is not a claim — it is a
  choice about what to pay attention to.
- **Theory as a hypothesis.** A claim that could turn out false. "If Russian lawfare
  is centrally directed, we should see legal instruments in place before the disputes
  they serve. Here is whether we do."

Supervisors say "theoretical framework" and usually mean the first. Examiners attack
you for not having the second. Both are legitimate; the failure is being vague about
which one you are doing at any given moment.

**Your thesis needs both, in different places.** Lens for slots 1 and 2. Hypotheses
for slot 3. Saying so explicitly — "in this chapter theory is doing X, in that
chapter it is doing Y" — is most of what a strong theory chapter consists of.

## 4. Slot 3 concretely: four rival accounts

These are the ones that can be wrong, so this is where theory earns its place. Each
is an answer to "what kind of behaviour is this?", and each predicts something
different.

1. **Strategy** — centrally directed, doctrinally anchored. Someone decided this.
2. **Organisational routine** — the MFA Legal Department produces legal arguments
   because that is its standing function. Patterned, but nobody decided anything.
3. **Practice** — an ingrained repertoire. Officials reach for certain legal moves
   the way a competent professional reaches for familiar tools, without deliberating.
4. **Inherited continuity** — the moves are transmitted from Soviet and imperial
   practice. Deep, but not designed.

Note that 2, 3 and 4 are all **"structured but not strategic"** — and they are
distinguishable from each other. That is the interesting finding space, and it only
opens up if you stop treating the question as strategy-versus-opportunism.

"Opportunism" is then the residual: what is left when none of the four patterns
holds.

## 5. Worked example — one fact, four theories

The point of this section is to make §2 concrete. The same observable fact, and what
each account says you should *additionally* find if it is the right one.

**The fact.** Russia ratified UNCLOS in 1997 and filed a declaration under Article
298 excluding certain categories of dispute — military activities among them — from
compulsory dispute settlement. In 2018 it seized three Ukrainian naval vessels at the
Kerch Strait. When Ukraine litigated, Russia argued the military-activities exclusion
applied. That exclusion was filed twenty-one years before the incident it covered.

On its own this fact proves nothing. Here is theory doing work:

| Account | What else you should find | Where to look |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** | The 1997 exclusions map onto Russia's foreseeable disputes; similar pre-positioning across other treaties; legal advisers involved in operational planning | The declaration's categories; comparable reservations elsewhere; delegation composition |
| **Organisational routine** | The declaration resembles other states' Article 298 declarations; timing tracks the ratification schedule, not geopolitics; no anticipation of anything specific | Other states' declarations (China, France); the ratification record |
| **Practice** | The *form* recurs everywhere while content varies — jurisdictional objections at similar intensity across UNCLOS, ECtHR, ICJ, investment arbitration; practitioners cannot state the rule they follow | Cross-forum comparison of procedural conduct |
| **Inherited continuity** | A direct genealogy from Soviet reservation practice, in the same categories | Soviet reservations to compromissory clauses |

That last row is checkable right now, and it probably comes out positive. The USSR
systematically reserved against compromissory clauses on ratification, declaring
itself not bound by them — and on 10 February 1989 the Presidium of the Supreme
Soviet withdrew those reservations for six human rights conventions, accepting ICJ
jurisdiction under them. So the sequence is: Soviet exclusion of compulsory
jurisdiction as standing practice → a narrow late-Soviet opening → post-Soviet Russia
excluding it again under Article 298.

If that genealogy holds, then Russia's jurisdictional conduct is **structured, deep,
and not a strategy** — it is an inherited administrative reflex. That is a real
finding, it is not something you could have reached by describing cases, and it came
from a theory telling you where to look. That is what theory is for.

## 5a. Correction: realism on law as an instrument of power

The three-slot split in §1 drew the line in the wrong place. I put it between realism
and non-realism. **The real line runs inside realism**, and getting this right
simplifies the whole framework.

- **Structural realism / neorealism** has almost nothing on law. Law is epiphenomenal,
  institutions "largely a reflection of the distribution of power". This is where the
  folk belief "realists don't do law" comes from.
- **Classical realism** has a great deal — because several of its founders were
  *international lawyers by training*, and wrote about law as their primary subject.

So slot 2 is not a departure from realism. **It is a return to classical realism.**

### The lawyers who became realists

- **Georg Schwarzenberger**, *Power Politics* (1941, rev. 1951). Called international
  law "power politics in disguise". A Weimar-trained jurist whose work, unlike other
  classical realists', focuses squarely on the nature of international law and its
  relation to power. **The most explicitly realist international lawyer there is, and
  the most underused.** Recovered in Carmen Chas, "The reality and power of
  international law: Georg Schwarzenberger's forgotten theory of International
  Relations", *Cooperation and Conflict* (2024) — which argues directly against the
  idea that realism cannot account for international law. Start here.
- **Hans Morgenthau** — doctoral work on the international judicial function;
  "Positivism, Functionalism, and International Law", *AJIL* 34 (1940); substantial
  chapters on international law in *Politics Among Nations*. The monograph is Oliver
  Jütersonke, *Morgenthau, Law and Realism* (CUP, 2010), reviewed in *The Review of
  Politics* under the title "Morgenthau, the Realist Jurist". See also William
  Scheuerman, *Hans Morgenthau: Realism and Beyond* (Polity, 2009).
- **John H. Herz** — also a lawyer, and the source of the "security dilemma". Casper
  Sylvest, "Realism and international law: the challenge of John H. Herz",
  *International Theory* 2:3 (2010), 410–445.
- **E. H. Carr**, *The Twenty Years' Crisis* (1939), especially Part Two ch. 4, "The
  Harmony of Interests". **Carr's argument is nearly identical to Koskenniemi's**: the
  dominant power presents its own particular interest as the universal interest, and
  law is the medium in which it does so. Carr's "have" and "have not" states — the
  privileged unwilling to renegotiate any part of an established advantage — is also
  the cleanest available frame for a revisionist-power thesis.
- **Carl Schmitt**, *The Nomos of the Earth*, and the critique of the discriminatory
  concept of war. Not a realist in the disciplinary sense, but the major thinker on
  law as spatial-political order — and directly relevant to a Russia case, since
  Schmittian themes (sovereignty, Großraum as sphere of influence, the critique of
  liberal universalism) circulate in Russian legal-political discourse.

### The bridge text

**Koskenniemi, *The Gentle Civilizer of Nations* (CUP, 2001), chapter 6: "Out of
Europe: Carl Schmitt, Hans Morgenthau, and the turn to 'international relations'".**

Koskenniemi himself wrote the definitive account of how realism emerged out of a
crisis in international legal thought — Morgenthau as a jurist whose turn to IR
followed the collapse of legal formalism. **This one chapter dissolves the supposed
gap between slot 1 and slot 2.** If you read one thing from this note, read this.

### Koskenniemi is not a realist — and the difference matters

He is a critical legal scholar: the structuralist / critical-legal-studies tradition,
with "hegemony" coming from Gramsci and Laclau rather than from Morgenthau. Calling
him a realist in a viva will get you corrected. But the deeper issue is substantive,
because the two traditions reach similar conclusions from opposite premises:

| | Why law tracks power | What kind of claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Carr, Schwarzenberger, Morgenthau** | States are self-interested; the system is anarchic; law reflects the interests of those who made it | Explanatory, about state motives |
| **Koskenniemi** | Legal argument is structurally indeterminate, so any outcome can be justified in legal terms; "hegemony" is the contest over whose particular preference gets to appear universal | About the structure of legal argument, not about motives |

**This is a live threat to your thesis, not a footnote.** If all legal argument is
hegemonic contestation, then Russia's is not special, "lawfare" is ubiquitous, and
your discrimination claim collapses. Koskenniemi gives you the most powerful account
of why law is a medium of power *and* the strongest reason to doubt that lawfare
picks out anything distinctive.

So your definition has to sort **within** the space Koskenniemi describes. Not "is
this hegemonic contestation?" — on his account everything is — but "which instances
carry the additional features that make lawfare a distinct kind of conduct?" Handling
this explicitly is one of the strongest things your first chapter can do.

### The named debate you should build slot 2 on

There is a decade-spanning exchange in the law journals on precisely "law as an
instrument of hegemonic power". It is more precise than "realism" and it is where
this argument actually happens:

- **Vagts, "Hegemonic International Law", *AJIL* 95 (2001)**
- **Alvarez, "Hegemonic International Law Revisited", *AJIL* 97 (2003)** — on the
  limits encountered by actors trying to use international law for cynical purposes.
  **That is your dependent variable, addressed directly.**
- **Krisch, "International Law in Times of Hegemony: Unequal Power and the Shaping of
  the International Legal Order", *EJIL* 16:3 (2005), 369–.** Argues that hegemony and
  international law are not irreconcilable: hegemons do not abandon law for political
  tools, they work through it.
- **Koskenniemi, "International law and hegemony: a reconfiguration", *CRIA* 17:2
  (2004)** sits inside this conversation.

### What this changes

- **Slots 1 and 2 merge.** Classical realism plus the hegemonic-international-law
  debate is one coherent theoretical home, not two frameworks bolted together.
- **Neoclassical realism becomes optional.** Keep it for the demand side if your
  department wants a contemporary framework — but you no longer need it, and dropping
  it removes the Legro & Moravcsik falsifiability exposure entirely. Classical realism
  does not make the positivist promises NCR is attacked for failing to keep.
- **Slot 3 is unchanged.** The four rival accounts still do the discriminating work,
  and that is still where the thesis is tested.

One consequence worth noticing: the paragraph in §8 now reads better as "I work in the
classical realist tradition, which — unlike structural realism — treated international
law as a central object of analysis," which is a stronger and more distinctive claim
than positioning yourself with neoclassical realism.

## 5b. Staying in political science

The constraint is real, but it is narrower than it feels. **It is about where the
explanandum sits, not about which sources you cite.** You can cite Koskenniemi, AJIL
and EJIL freely. What makes a thesis political science rather than law is:

- the dependent variable is **state behaviour**, not the content or validity of legal
  rules;
- the claims are **explanatory about politics**, not doctrinal about what the law
  requires;
- the method is social-scientific — case comparison, process tracing, content
  analysis — not doctrinal interpretation.

### The one diagnostic test

> **Does your thesis ever have to assert that Russia was legally wrong?**

If yes, you have drifted into law. A political science thesis says: *Russia advanced
argument X in forum Y at time Z; here is what that tells us about how Russia behaves.*
The legal validity of the argument is not your business — except as a **datum**, and
an informative one: a weak argument advanced anyway, at cost, tells you something
about behaviour, which is your actual subject.

This should be a relief. **You never have to adjudicate a legal question.** That is
the boundary, and it is easy to hold.

### What this means for §5a

Keep Carr and Morgenthau — they are IR canon and no political scientist will blink.
Keep Schwarzenberger if he earns his place. But **demote the hegemonic-international-
law debate (Vagts, Alvarez, Krisch) from framework to warrant**: one paragraph in
chapter 1 establishing that law-as-instrument-of-power is a serious position in legal
scholarship too, then move on.

Because there is a well-developed **political science** literature doing exactly the
job slot 2 needs, written by people in politics departments.

### The political science literature for slot 2

**Legitimacy as a resource.** **Ian Hurd — Political Science, Northwestern —
*After Anarchy: Legitimacy and Power in the United Nations Security Council*
(Princeton UP, 2007).** Won the ISA's Chadwick Alger Award. Its central finding is
tailor-made for your thesis: **when states use the Council's legitimacy for their own
purposes, they reaffirm its stature and end up contributing to its authority.** That
is a causal mechanism explaining why instrumental use of an institution is not simply
corrosive — it is self-reinforcing. Hurd also deliberately joins realist and
constructivist arguments, which gives you cover on both flanks. See also Bukovansky,
*Legitimacy and Power Politics* (Princeton UP, 2002).

**Rhetoric as coercion — the mechanism, and the methodological escape hatch.**
**Krebs & Jackson, "Twisting Tongues and Twisting Arms: The Power of Political
Rhetoric", *European Journal of International Relations* 13:1 (2007).** Their move is
to **abandon persuasion** — theoretically and methodologically unprovable — and model
rhetoric as *coercion*: manoeuvring an opponent into a position where they cannot
publicly sustain their objection.

**This may be the single most useful import available to you.** It gives a political
science answer to "what does legal argument actually do?" that requires no legal
theory *and no claim about sincerity*. You do not need to know what Russian officials
believed. That dissolves the hardest evidentiary problem in the whole project.

With it: **Schimmelfennig, "The Community Trap", *International Organization* 55:1
(2001)** on rhetorical action and entrapment; and **Scherzinger, "'Acting under
Chapter 7': rhetorical entrapment, rhetorical hollowing, and the authorization of
force in the UN Security Council, 1995–2017", *International Relations* (2023)** — a
political science article applying entrapment to Security Council legal authorisation
practice. **Read that one as a template for your institutional chapters.**

**Regime complexity and forum shopping.** **Karen Alter — Political Science,
Northwestern — & Meunier, "The Politics of International Regime Complexity",
*Perspectives on Politics* 7:1 (2009)**; Alter & Raustiala, "The Rise of International
Regime Complexity", *Annual Review of Law and Social Science* (2018); Alter, *The New
Terrain of International Law* (Princeton UP, 2014).

This literature names the cross-institutional strategies directly: **forum shopping,
regime shifting, and strategic inconsistency.** Your four arenas *are* a regime
complex. "Russia selects venues strategically" is a forum-shopping claim with a mature
political science vocabulary already attached, and "strategic inconsistency" —
deliberately generating conflicting obligations — is a lawfare tactic that already has
a political science name. **This reframes your entire empirical design in
discipline-native terms.**

**Institutional design — the political science home for pre-positioning.**
**Koremenos, Lipson & Snidal, "The Rational Design of International Institutions",
*International Organization* 55:4 (2001)**, and the CUP volume. Five design
dimensions — membership, scope, centralization, control, and **flexibility**.

Reservations, opt-outs and escape clauses are *designed flexibility* in this
literature. So your Article 298 case stops being "Russia pre-positioned legally" and
becomes "**Russia exploited designed flexibility**" — the same observation in
political science vocabulary, with a built-in comparative baseline: which states use
which flexibility provisions, when, and how much.

**Compliance and selection — which solves the methods problem in `docs/06` §2.**
**Downs, Rocke & Barsoom, "Is the Good News about Compliance Good News about
Cooperation?", *International Organization* (1996)**, and **von Stein, "Do Treaties
Constrain or Screen? Selection Bias and Treaty Compliance", *American Political
Science Review* 99:4 (2005), 611–622.**

This is political science's own literature on exactly the selection problem flagged in
`docs/06`: do states comply because the commitment binds them, or because they signed
commitments matching what they would have done anyway? Von Stein finds the
unobservable conditions leading states to commit substantially shape compliance.
**Citing this is the discipline-respectable way to handle your negative-cases issue**,
and it shows you know your methods literature.

**And keep Krasner**, *Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy* (Princeton UP, 1999) —
political science, and still the most citable statement that rules are systematically
violated *and* systematically invoked. Ikenberry, *After Victory* / *Liberal
Leviathan*, and Gruber, *Ruling the World* (Princeton UP, 2000) on institutions as
power bargains, if you need the order-level frame.

### The framework, restated in political science

> Russia is a revisionist power in a dense regime complex. Legal argument is a cheap
> instrument because legitimacy is a resource operating partly independently of
> material power (Hurd). It works not by persuading anyone but by rhetorically
> constraining opponents (Krebs & Jackson). The regime complex supplies multiple
> venues and the option of strategic inconsistency (Alter). Whether Russia's
> exploitation of this is centrally directed, bureaucratically routine, an ingrained
> practice, or an inherited repertoire is my empirical question.

No doctrinal claim anywhere in it, every citation from a politics department, and more
specific than "realism". That is the framework.

### One honest cost

The political science literature on international law is **weak on the content of
legal argument**. Krebs & Jackson do not care what an argument says, only what
position it forces an opponent into. If part of your contribution depends on the
specific texture of Russian legal argumentation — which is what Mälksoo's material
gives you — you will need the law literature for that.

That is fine, but **name it yourself**: the thesis is IR, with an empirical base in
legal materials. Hurd, Alter and Simmons all work that way. Declaring it in the
introduction is much better than having an examiner declare it for you.

## 6. The test to apply to any theory you consider

> **If I delete this theory from the thesis, what breaks?**

If the answer is "nothing — the empirical chapters read the same," it is decoration
and you should cut it. Applied to your candidates:

- **Delete realism** → you lose the answer to "why is Russia doing any of this", and
  the thesis becomes legal description with no account of motive. Keep it, small.
- **Delete the IR–IL theory (slot 2)** → your concept of lawfare loses its warrant.
  If legal argument is not a resource of power, there is nothing to study. **This is
  load-bearing.**
- **Delete the slot 3 rivals** → your final chapter becomes an assertion about
  Russian intentions with no way to adjudicate it. **Load-bearing.**
- **Delete Mearsheimer specifically** → nothing breaks. Cut.

## 7. Chapter 1 is not a theory chapter

Worth saying plainly, because it may be part of the confusion. "Lawfare is a real
academic term and it can discriminate" is **concept formation**, which is a distinct
methodological literature, not IR theory:

- Sartori, "Concept Misformation in Comparative Politics", *American Political
  Science Review* (1970)
- Collier & Mahon, "Conceptual 'Stretching' Revisited: Adapting Categories in
  Comparative Analysis", *APSR* (1993) — on family-resemblance and radial categories,
  directly relevant if lawfare turns out not to have one single defining attribute

Don't try to theorise that chapter. **Operationalise it**: state the conditions,
then show the concept sorting real cases in and out. Theory enters only at one point
— your definition has to rest on some claim about why legal argument is politically
consequential, and that claim comes from slot 2.

## 8. The paragraph to say out loud

If someone asks what your theoretical framework is:

> "Realism tells me why Russia wants to contest an international order it cannot
> overturn by force. It doesn't tell me why *law* is one of the instruments it reaches
> for — for that I use the IR–international law literature that treats legal argument
> as a resource of legitimacy operating partly independently of material power.
> Together those give me a concept of lawfare with a reason to exist. My empirical
> question is then whether Russia's lawfare is centrally directed, bureaucratically
> routine, an ingrained practice, or an inherited repertoire — four accounts that
> predict different observable patterns, which is what makes my case comparisons a
> test rather than a description."

Three theoretical moves, three jobs, and an empirical question that could come out
more than one way. That is a framework. It was never going to be one theory.

## 9. If you only do one thing next

Take three or four cases you already know well and fill in the §5 table for each:
what you observe, and what each of the four accounts predicts you would *also* see.
Where the predictions differ, you have a research task. Where they don't, that case
cannot discriminate and is only illustration.

That exercise is the theory chapter. Writing it afterwards is transcription.
