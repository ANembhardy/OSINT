# Connecting the two mechanisms to political science theory

On the draft article *Restraint and Recognition* (v4). The article is a
concept-formation piece and is already closer to political science than the
"theory problem" suggested — Barnett & Finnemore, Farrell & Newman, Sartori,
Goertz, Adcock & Collier are all in the bibliography. What is missing is not a
framework bolted on top. It is an **account of why each mechanism works**. The
article specifies the two paths structurally and then treats their operation as
a brute fact.

Each section below names the theory that supplies the missing account.

---

## 1. Restraint → commitment-as-vulnerability and asymmetric conflict

The restraint mode says the target is impeded by its own accepted commitment
while the actor is not. In political science that is the legal specification of
a well-developed argument.

- **Mack, "Why Big Nations Lose Small Wars: The Politics of Asymmetric
  Conflict", *World Politics* 27:2 (1975), 175–200.** Power asymmetry generates
  interest asymmetry: the greater the material gap, the less resolute and *more
  politically vulnerable* the strong actor. Big nations lose because frustrated
  publics or countervailing elites force withdrawal short of military defeat.
- **Arreguín-Toft, *How the Weak Win Wars: A Theory of Asymmetric Conflict*
  (CUP, 2005).** Materially inferior actors prevail in roughly 30% of asymmetric
  conflicts, with success rising sharply where the weaker party shifts the
  contest off conventional ground.

Restraint-mode lawfare **is** that shift, onto legal ground. The constraint is
not material; it is a rule the target accepts and its own domestic accountability
enforces.

This also vindicates §6's claim that the restraint mode is not confined to armed
conflict, and §12's observation that its non-kinetic instances connect better to
the hybrid-threats literature than the battlefield cases do. **Asymmetric
conflict theory was never about battlefields — it was about political
tolerance.** That is why the mode travels to asylum systems, navigational
regimes and consensus rules without strain.

### The claim worth making explicitly

The credible-commitment literature — Schelling on tying hands, Fearon on
audience costs — treats self-binding as a *source* of bargaining power: a
commitment you cannot escape is one your opponent must believe.

**The restraint mode is that mechanism inverted.** The same self-binding that
makes a commitment credible makes it exploitable by an adversary who has not
bound itself. Credibility and vulnerability are the same property viewed from
the two sides.

That is a theoretical claim, not a mapping, and it is worth stating as one. It
also explains the distributional fact the article notices but does not theorise:
the restraint mode bites hardest on liberal democracies, because their
commitments are credible precisely to the degree that they are domestically
enforced.

## 2. Recognition → parasitism on institutional authority

The recognition mode depends on third parties treating a resource as
authoritative. **The article asserts that they do and never says why they can.**
That is the largest unexplained premise in the piece, and it has a standard
answer.

- **Barnett & Finnemore, *Rules for the World: International Organizations in
  Global Politics* (Cornell UP, 2004).** Already cited in the draft but not used
  theoretically. International organisations possess authority derived from
  delegation, rational-legal character and expertise — authority with effects
  partly independent of member states' power. **This is the account of why a red
  notice or a treaty-body determination can move border officials, banks and
  foreign ministries.** Promote it from the reference list into section 6.
- **Hurd, *After Anarchy: Legitimacy and Power in the United Nations Security
  Council* (Princeton UP, 2007).** Legitimacy as a resource, worked out on the
  Council specifically.

### The structural insight: authority is a common-pool resource

- **Ostrom, *Governing the Commons* (CUP, 1990).**

Institutional authority behaves like a common-pool resource: non-excludable
among members, and depletable by abuse. The arrest-notice case is a textbook
appropriation problem — each abusive notice captures a private benefit while
distributing the cost of degraded trust across every member that relies on the
system.

So **condition (ii) is a free-riding condition** in common-pool terms, and
Ostrom supplies the vocabulary for when such resources degrade and when they
sustain themselves under appropriation pressure. That is precisely the question
§12 leaves open (see §5 below).

## 3. Non-reciprocity → Keohane, and a refinement of Krasner

- **Keohane, "Reciprocity in International Relations", *International
  Organization* 40:1 (1986), 1–27.** Reciprocity is the mechanism sustaining
  cooperation absent hierarchical authority or centralised enforcement, and it
  works in part by **deterring exploitation**. Condition (ii) is a specification
  of failed reciprocity, narrowed to a single resource. **This is the best single
  citation for the condition.**

  Keohane's **specific / diffuse** distinction may earn its keep beyond the
  citation. Specific reciprocity: named partners, equivalent items, delimited
  sequence. Diffuse: imprecise equivalence, partners as a group, loose sequence.
  Recognition-mode extraction is a failure of *diffuse* reciprocity — which is
  exactly why it is hard to detect and harder to police, and therefore why the
  "same resource" requirement is doing the work of making it detectable at all.
  That connection would strengthen §8's defence of the requirement considerably.

- **Krasner, *Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy* (Princeton UP, 1999).**
  Worth engaging directly, because §8 already makes Krasner's point — "every
  state that benefits from international law also violates some of it" — and then
  disciplines it by requiring the refused burden to be *the condition of the
  claimed benefit, under the same resource*. **Condition (ii) is a refinement of
  organized hypocrisy into something that discriminates.** Saying so positions
  the article against a canonical political science text and shows the condition
  is not a restatement.

- Olson, *The Logic of Collective Action*, for the free-riding baseline.

## 4. The identification rule → Barnett & Duvall

**Barnett & Duvall, "Power in International Politics", *International
Organization* 59:1 (2005), 39–75.** Four forms — compulsory, institutional,
structural, productive — argued against a discipline that privileges the first
and so misses how global outcomes are produced.

Section 7's rule — *identify the effect first, then ask what produced it* — is a
power-analytic move of exactly this kind. Rough mapping:

- **Recognition ≈ institutional power.** A affects B through institutional
  mediation, at a distance, diffusely.
- **Restraint ≈ closest to structural power**, the target's own position and
  commitments constituting the constraint — though it carries a compulsory
  element too, since the actor arranges the circumstances.

Be honest that the fit is imperfect: the distinction **cuts across** their
taxonomy rather than sitting inside it, and that is arguable as a contribution.
But engage it, because this is among the most-assigned articles in US IR graduate
training. **A politics reviewer will ask how this relates to Barnett & Duvall.**
Have the answer ready rather than discovering the question in a referee report.

## 5. The tension worth owning — and the PhD's real question

Section 8 assumes that extraction imposes costs on the resource: *"the
institution and its members bear the cost of maintaining the credibility on which
the deployment relies, and the actor does not."*

**Hurd's central finding in *After Anarchy* is close to the opposite.** When
states use the Security Council's legitimacy for their own purposes, they
reaffirm its stature and end up contributing to its authority. Instrumental use
is self-reinforcing rather than corrosive.

Both cannot be generally true. That opens the question:

> **Does authority extraction deplete the authority it borrows, or reinforce it —
> and under what conditions does each occur?**

The likely answer is conditional: on whether the extraction is publicly legible
*as* abuse, on whether the institution can sanction it, on Ostrom's conditions
for common-pool maintenance. Testable on the arrest-notice record, on Security
Council designation practice, on the ECtHR sequence.

**This is a better core for the PhD than "is Russian lawfare strategic or
opportunistic?"** It is a causal question with a real dependent variable and
policy stakes; it is answerable from institutional records; the article's
framework is what makes it askable; and — decisively, given the difficulty
identified in `docs/06` — **it requires proving nothing about Russian
intentions.** Russia becomes the case rather than the puzzle.

Note also that the article's closing paragraph already gestures at an
opportunism-based empirical programme ("patterns that opportunism cannot
explain"). The depletion question is the stronger version of that ambition,
because it does not depend on ruling out a mental state.

## 6. Three gaps, in priority order

1. **Why recognition happens.** §2 above. Currently an unexplained premise.
2. **Whether extraction depletes.** §5 above. Currently an unexamined assumption
   that a canonical text contradicts.
3. **Why these actors and why now.** Out of scope for a concept-formation
   article, but one paragraph acknowledging it protects the flank, and it is
   where classical realism or neoclassical realism belongs (`docs/07` §5a).

## 7. One critical point, before a reviewer makes it

**The "same resource" requirement does unequal work in the two modes.**

In recognition mode it is tight and the article demonstrates it well — the
sanctioned-shipping case names the benefit (freedom of navigation, narrow right
of visit) and names the conditions (single flag, genuine link, safety
conventions) under the same regime. That is exactly the procedure §8 promises.

In restraint mode it is looser. Perfidy works because the emblem's protection is
*expressly* conditioned on not misusing it — which is why §9 rightly calls it
striking corroboration. But human shields is weaker on the same test:
proportionality does not charge "do not use shields" as its price. They are
distinct rules within one body of law.

So a reviewer can say: in restraint-mode cases the resource has been identified
broadly (IHL's protective regime as a whole) to make the conditionality hold,
immediately after §7 insisted resource identification must be tight. Since §8's
sixth observation leans hard on same-resource closure as what makes the
definition operationalisable, this is load-bearing.

Three available responses:

- **(a)** Argue the protective regime is one resource with internal
  conditionality — defensible for emblems and protected sites, harder across
  distinction and proportionality generally.
- **(b)** Concede the test is weaker in restraint mode and say why that is
  tolerable — for instance that restraint-mode cases are independently secured by
  the breach requirement in condition (i)(a), which has no analogue in (b).
  **Probably the honest option.**
- **(c)** Restrict restraint-mode cases to express conditionality. This costs you
  human shields, the founding paradigm, and so fails the article's own
  preservation requirement.

Also worth a second look: the asylum-saturation exclusion turns on a non-party
claiming no benefit. A non-party arguably does benefit from the refugee regime's
existence, through burden-shifting onto parties. The exclusion is defensible —
that is not a benefit *of the resource's operation* in the required sense — but
say so, rather than leaving it to inference.

## 8. Two corrections to earlier advice in this repo

**Krebs & Jackson on rhetorical coercion is less central than `docs/07` §5b
suggested.** The article excludes standalone discourse on mechanism grounds and
accepts Schu's second arm, so rhetorical coercion is the theory of the category
being ruled out. It keeps a secondary role: it is the theory of how a *target*
exploits the inconsistency that condition (ii) identifies — i.e. counter-lawfare,
and how non-reciprocity becomes politically costly once named.

**Alter's "strategic inconsistency" is not condition (ii), and the names will be
confused.** Alter & Meunier mean creating conflicting obligations *across
regimes*; condition (ii) means inconsistency *within one actor's practice toward
one resource*. Distinguish them explicitly in a footnote. Regime complexity stays
relevant for a different reason: the recognition mode presupposes multiple venues
holding authority worth borrowing, and venue selection within it is forum
shopping.

None of this requires a new framework. It requires promoting theory the article
already half-contains, and naming the mechanisms in the vocabulary the discipline
already uses for them.
