## 1. UN voting alignment — measured

**This section is now computed, not quoted.** Sources: the Dag Hammarskjold
Library General Assembly voting dataset, version 5 (Feb 2026; 947,434 rows,
resolutions 1/1946 to 80/246/2025) and the UNDL Security Council export
(41,349 rows, 2,815 ballots, 1946 to 30 Jan 2026). Both are committed under
`data/raw/`. Reproduce with `make ga` and `make unsc`; the numbers below are
guarded by tests in `tests/test_unsc.py`.

### 1.1 The scope trap in both datasets

Neither export contains **failed or vetoed drafts**. Every Security Council
ballot carries an `S/RES/` symbol, which a draft only receives on adoption; the
GA codebook says outright that "drafts that failed to be adopted are not
included". Verified by probing for the September 2022 Ukraine, March 2024 Gaza
and October 2011 Syria double vetoes — none appear.

**Consequence: no veto analysis is possible from this data.** A veto count
computed from these files returns approximately zero. The veto table in §1.4 is
hand-maintained from the UNSC veto list, separately.

### 1.2 General Assembly, 1992–2025 (PRC era, post-Soviet)

| Subset | n | Russia–China agreement |
|---|---|---|
| All recorded votes | 2,751 | **71.1%** |
| Contested (≥10% minority) | 1,457 | **58.4%** |

**The base-rate control changes the story completely:**

| Dyad | Agreement (contested) |
|---|---|
| China–Pakistan | 83.3% |
| **Global South sample, mean pairwise** | **82.2%** |
| China–India | 78.2% |
| China–South Africa | 70.4% |
| China–Brazil | 66.1% |
| **Russia–China** | **58.4%** |
| Russia–Iran | 53.7% |
| Russia–India | 51.7% |

Russia and China agree with each other **less than the average pair of Global
South states agree with each other**, and China agrees substantially more with
India and Pakistan than it does with Russia. On General Assembly voting alone,
"Russia–China alignment" is not a meaningful signal — it is *below* the ambient
level of coordination in the caucus both belong to.

The reason is visible in the per-country numbers: **Russia is the isolate, not
the pair.** Russia's agreement with everyone is low. China votes like a fairly
mainstream member of the Global South.

### 1.3 The published "77% → 67%" decline, tested

The MERICS/SCEEUS figure — convergence falling from ~77% (2018) to ~67% (2024) —
reproduces almost exactly on the **raw** series, and largely dissolves on the
contested subset:

| Session | Year | Raw | Contested |
|---|---|---|---|
| 72 | 2018 | 77.9% | 66.7% |
| 73 | 2019 | 78.7% | 75.0% |
| 76 | 2022 | 78.8% | 72.0% |
| 78 | 2024 | 66.7% | 64.9% |
| 80 | 2025 | 77.4% | 68.3% |

Raw falls 11.2 points from 2018 to 2024; contested falls **1.8 points**. Most of
the reported "friends moving apart" effect is a change in the mix of
near-unanimous resolutions, not a change in how the two behave when a vote is
actually contested. And the series does not continue downward — session 80 (2025)
is back to 77.4% raw.

**What they actually vote opposite on.** 128 opposed votes on contested texts
since 1992, and the recent ones are overwhelmingly one-directional — *Russia
votes no, China votes yes*:

- nuclear disarmament (A/RES/80/24, 80/49, 79/47)
- cluster munitions (80/52, 79/58) and anti-personnel mines (80/35)
- nuclear-weapon-free zones (80/31); ICJ advisory opinion on nuclear weapons (80/33)
- Human Rights Council reports (79/157, 80/189)

The one running the other way is the **death-penalty moratorium** (79/179:
Russia yes, China no). So the divergence is not China drifting from Russia — it
is **Russia defecting from a disarmament and human-rights consensus that China
still votes with.**

### 1.4 Security Council, 1992–2026 (adopted resolutions only)

2,039 adopted ballots in scope; 242 (11.9%) non-unanimous. On a 15-member body a
single abstention is 6.7%, so the Assembly's 10%-minority rule is the wrong
instrument — any recorded dissent counts as contested here.

| Subset | n | Russia–China agreement |
|---|---|---|
| All adopted | 2,038 | **95.0%** |
| Contested (non-unanimous) | 242 | **57.9%** |

Baselines on the same contested ballots:

| Dyad | Agreement |
|---|---|
| UK–France | 96.3% |
| US–UK | 92.6% |
| US–France | 89.6% |
| **Russia–China** | **57.9%** |
| US–China | 43.4% |
| US–Russia | 33.9% |

**The Western P3 is a far tighter voting bloc than Russia–China.** On adopted
Council resolutions the two are closer to each other than either is to
Washington, but nowhere near the cohesion of the Atlantic three.

Two structural findings:

1. **Russia and China have never once voted on opposite sides of an adopted
   Council resolution** in this period — zero opposed votes in 2,038 ballots.
   Every one of the 102 divergences is a *partial split*: one votes yes, the
   other abstains. Whatever the disagreement, neither will hand the other a
   public defeat.
2. **Russia is the more frequent abstainer** — 61% of contested ballots versus
   China's 50%, against 8% (US), 4% (France), 2% (UK). And in **63% of the
   splits it is China voting yes while Russia abstains**, not the reverse. The
   common framing of a committed Russia and a hedging China is backwards on this
   evidence.

Agreement by period (contested): 29.6% (1990–94) → 75.0% (2000–04) → 57.7%
(2020–24) → 84.2% (2025–26, n=19). The partnership's Council behaviour converged
sharply after 2000, drifted through the 2010s, and has tightened again very
recently — though the last bucket is small.

### 1.5 Issue-contingency: the sharpest result in the data

Alignment is not uniform across topics; it is patterned:

| Emergency special session | Subject | Russia–China agreement |
|---|---|---|
| ES-10 | Occupied Palestinian territory | **92.3%** |
| ES-11 | Ukraine | **22.2%** |

Every ES-11 vote, 2022–2025:

| Date | Resolution | Russia | China |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2022-03-02 | ES-11/1 | No | Abstain |
| 2022-03-24 | ES-11/2 | No | Abstain |
| 2022-04-07 | ES-11/3 | No | **No** |
| 2022-10-12 | ES-11/4 | No | Abstain |
| 2022-11-14 | ES-11/5 | No | **No** |
| 2023-02-23 | ES-11/6 | No | Abstain |
| 2025-02-24 | ES-11/7 | No | Abstain |
| 2025-02-24 | ES-11/8 | No | Abstain |
| 2025-12-03 | ES-11/9 | No | Abstain |

China abstained on seven of nine and joined Russia in voting no on exactly two —
**ES-11/3 (suspending Russia from the Human Rights Council) and ES-11/5 (the
reparations / register of damage mechanism)**. Both are texts that set an
*institutional precedent* — expelling a member from a UN body, and creating a
compensation mechanism against a state — rather than texts condemning the
invasion.

That is the operative rule, and it is testable: **China protects precedents that
could later be turned against it, and abstains on everything that is merely about
Russia.** Meanwhile on Palestine the two vote together almost perfectly. Any
claim about "Russia–China alignment" that does not specify the issue class is
close to meaningless.

### 1.6 The 2021–2026 window

Reproduce with `make ga-window` / `make unsc-window`, or
`python -m rcwatch ga --since 2021-01-01`. Coverage note: the GA dataset ends
18 December 2025 and the SC export ends 30 January 2026, so "2021–2026" is five
full years plus one month of Council business — 550 GA resolutions and 252
adopted Council ballots.

**Both chambers show the dyad tighter in this window than over the long run:**

| Measure | 1992–2025/26 | 2021–2026 |
|---|---|---|
| GA, all recorded votes | 71.1% | **72.9%** |
| GA, contested | 58.4% | **68.1%** |
| SC, all adopted | 95.0% | 88.9% |
| SC, contested | 57.9% | **63.6%** |

A near-10-point rise on contested GA votes is the largest movement in the data.
So on the recent window the "friends moving apart" reading is not merely
overstated — it points the wrong way.

**But the base-rate control still inverts the headline.** On contested GA votes
in the same window:

| Dyad | 2021–2026 | (1992–2025) |
|---|---|---|
| **Global South sample, mean pairwise** | **79.3%** | 82.2% |
| China–Pakistan | 78.1% | 83.3% |
| US–UK | 73.5% | 68.7% |
| **Russia–China** | **68.1%** | 58.4% |
| China–India | 66.8% | 78.2% |
| Russia–Iran | 65.3% | 53.7% |
| China–Brazil | 59.8% | 66.1% |
| Russia–India | 53.2% | 51.7% |

Russia–China remains **below** the ambient Global South level (79.3%). What has
changed is the surrounding structure, not the dyad's exceptionalism:
China–India has fallen 11 points while Russia–China rose 10, so the two now sit
close together — Beijing is about as aligned with New Delhi as with Moscow.
Russia–Iran rose 12 points.

The sharpest movement is in the Council, and it is not about Russia and China at
all: **US–Russia agreement on contested adopted resolutions collapses from 33.9%
across the full series to 16.9% in this window**, and US–China falls to 36.4%.
The Western P3 stays cohesive (UK–France 97.4%, US–UK 92.2%). The dyad looks
more aligned partly because the alternative alignments have decayed faster.

**Russia is the rejectionist; China tracks the majority.** This is starker in the
window than over the long run:

- *General Assembly*, 101 contested splits: Russia takes the more negative
  position in **95** of them (Russia no / China abstain 37; Russia no / China yes
  31; Russia abstain / China yes 27), against 5 the other way.
- *Security Council*, 28 contested splits: **24 are China yes / Russia abstain**,
  3 the reverse. Russia abstains on **81%** of contested adopted resolutions in
  this window; China 55%; the US 9%, France and the UK 4%.

**All 33 opposed GA votes, by topic** — and 31 of the 33 are Russia no / China yes:

| Topic | Opposed votes |
|---|---|
| Nuclear disarmament | 10 |
| Other nuclear (incl. ICJ advisory opinion, effects research) | 6 |
| Cluster munitions | 5 |
| Nuclear-weapon-free zones | 4 |
| Human Rights Council reports | 3 |
| Death penalty moratorium (**Russia yes / China no**) | 2 |
| Anti-personnel mines | 1 |
| Other | 2 |

Arms control and human-rights machinery account for 29 of 33. The only texts
where China is the more negative party are the death-penalty resolutions — a
domestic-legal position, not a strategic one.

**Year by year, contested:**

| Year | GA session | GA contested | SC contested (n) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 76 | 72.0% | 55.6% (9) |
| 2022 | 77 | 69.5% | 66.7% (18) |
| 2023 | 78 | 64.9% | 53.3% (15) |
| 2024 | 79 | 68.3% | 50.0% (16) |
| 2025 | 80 | 68.3% | 82.4% (17) |
| 2026 | — | — | 100% (2) |

The GA series is flat-to-slightly-up across the war years. The Council series dips
through 2023–24 and jumps in 2025 — worth watching, but 17 and 2 ballots
respectively are small samples and the 2026 figure should not be quoted at all.

**Issue-contingency is absolute in this window.** Emergency special sessions:
ES-10 (occupied Palestinian territory), 7 resolutions, **100% agreement**;
ES-11 (Ukraine), 9 resolutions, **22.2%**. The same two governments, the same
five years, at opposite ends of the scale depending on the file.


## 2. Joint statements and third countries

**Form matters as much as content.** The strongest documentary evidence of
coordination is not the joint statement itself but the **joint letter to the
Secretary-General**: a bilateral text co-signed by both Permanent Representatives
and given a UN document symbol. Three are catalogued in
`data/seed/joint_documents.csv` (UNDL records 3908034, 4085306, 4089163),
covering global governance (Guilin, 2021), global strategic stability (Moscow,
8 May 2025) and the authority of international law (Moscow, 8 May 2025,
circulated 10 July 2025). This channel is under-examined and is trivially
monitorable — a standing UNDL query would catch each new one within days.

**Beijing, 20 May 2026 — the current baseline text.** Putin's 25th visit to
China produced *two* statements, and the split is deliberate: one on
strengthening bilateral strategic coordination and good-neighbourliness, one on
"advocating a multipolar world and a new type of international relations". The
second is the order-building document. The visit was staged on a triple
anniversary — 30 years of the strategic partnership, 25 years of the Treaty of
Good-Neighbourliness, and the opening of a China–Russia "Year of Education".

**By region:**

- **Arctic** — the most concrete third-party coordination of 2026. A joint action
  plan on the Northern Sea Route targets **20 million tonnes of cargo by 2030**
  and covers Arctic-class vessels, shared navigation systems, joint crew training
  and ice/meteorological data exchange. At least six Chinese shipping companies
  were expected to make 50+ voyages in the 2026 season; seven tankers carrying
  roughly 6 million barrels of Russian crude were transiting as of 11 August.
  Chinese Vice Premier Ding Xuexiang was in Vladivostok for the Eastern Economic
  Forum on 3 September 2026 pressing the same theme. Note the framing shift:
  after the Hormuz closure, the NSR is being sold as a **chokepoint-substitution
  route**, not merely an Arctic development project. That reframing is new in 2026
  and is the thing to watch.
- **Pacific** — "Joint Sea-2026" ran from 6 July off Qingdao, followed by a joint
  patrol: the task group left Qingdao on 13 July and transited the **Miyako,
  Etorofu and Soya straits**, operating in the western Pacific, the Sea of Okhotsk
  and the Sea of Japan before arriving in Vladivostok. Composition was modest
  (PLAN destroyers *Kaifeng* and *Anshan* plus replenishment ship *Kekexilihu*;
  Russian frigate *Ryzhy*). The significance is not tonnage — it is that the
  patrol runs under a **standing annual cooperation plan**, i.e. institutionalised
  rather than episodic, and that the route deliberately threads all three
  chokepoints of the first island chain.
- **Belarus** — Minsk is best read as a *separately managed* relationship, not a
  trilateral. Lukashenko met Xi in Beijing in June 2026; China frames ties as an
  "all-weather comprehensive strategic partnership" (since 2022) at a "historic
  peak", with 2026–27 designated Years of Industrial Cooperation. Lukashenko's own
  line is telling: China proposed working "according to the principle of your work
  with Russia — a region with regions", and he has publicly insisted his dialogue
  with Washington "is not against Russia or China". Belarus is diversifying
  patrons within the bloc rather than serving as a joint Russia–China project.
- **Central Asia** — this is where the "coordination" frame is weakest. The public
  choreography is strong (SCO summit in Bishkek, 31 Aug–1 Sep 2026, 25th
  anniversary; Putin–Xi bilateral on the margins; Putin offering to make the
  bilateral visa-free regime permanent). The substance is a **functional division
  of labour under strain**: Russia retains the security and political role, China
  holds the economic one, with friction over the Middle Corridor, which routes
  around Russia. One 2026 assessment puts it well — Russia's position is not
  collapsing but *hollowing*: treaty commitments and basing agreements intact,
  military credibility and economic competitiveness depleting. Expect the division
  of labour to be the first thing to fail if it fails anywhere.

## 3. Diplomatic contact

Run `make contacts` for the live numbers off `data/seed/meetings.csv`. From the
current (**incomplete** — see caveat below) table, leader level 2022–2026:

- 10 recorded leader contacts, **half dedicated visits, half summit pull-asides**;
- host split 6 China / 2 Russia / 2 third-country, i.e. a **reciprocity ratio of
  0.33** — Putin travels to Xi far more than the reverse;
- 0.8 documents signed per leader contact.

Xi and Putin had met **43 times since 2013** as of May 2024, and several times
since. The rhythm since 2022 is roughly: one dedicated state visit per year, plus
two to three multilateral pull-asides, plus commemorative set-pieces (Moscow
Victory Day, May 2025, Xi as guest of honour; Beijing's 80th-anniversary parade,
3 September 2025, with Putin and Kim Jong Un alongside Xi).

Below leader level the machinery is more revealing than the summits:

- **Foreign ministers meet on a fixed cycle**, and on 14 April 2026 in Beijing
  Lavrov and Wang Yi signed the **2026 inter-ministry consultation plan** — an
  annual instrument that pre-schedules coordination rather than reacting to
  events. They met again on 24 July 2026 at Cholpon-Ata on the SCO CFM margins.
- **Security-council-level strategic security consultations** (Patrushev/Shoigu –
  Wang Yi) run as a separate channel from the MFA track.

**Caveat, stated plainly:** the seed table is a floor, not a census. Known
meetings — the 2024 Astana SCO and Kazan BRICS summits among them — are not yet
recorded, and four rows are `pending_primary`. Per-year counts should not be
quoted until the table is completed from the Kremlin schedule and PRC MFA
activity pages. `make audit` lists exactly what is outstanding.

## 4. Shared positions

Convergent, and increasingly institutionalised rather than merely rhetorical:

- **Order framing** — multipolarity, "genuine multilateralism", the central role
  of the UN, opposition to "unilateral hegemony". Wang Yi's April 2026 framing —
  unilateral hegemony inflicting increasing harm, global governance under profound
  adjustment — is the standard formula.
- **Strategic stability** — jointly circulated at the UN, May 2025.
- **Cyber and information sovereignty** — both champion state authority over the
  domestic digital environment; both used the UN cybercrime convention
  negotiations to push a territorial-state model of cyberspace, with some success.
- **AI governance** — the 2026 statements commit both to keeping AI from becoming
  "a geopolitical tool for certain countries to maintain hegemony", and they have
  now built an institution: the **World Artificial Intelligence Cooperation
  Organization (WAICO)**, Shanghai-based, reportedly 29 founding members. *(Only
  weak sourcing was available for WAICO membership numbers — verify before use.)*
- **Iran** — the April 2026 Hormuz veto is the clearest joint operational position
  on a live conflict since Syria.

Where they do **not** converge: Ukraine (China withholds endorsement and abstains),
the death penalty, humanitarian arms control, and — under the surface — the
distribution of influence in Central Asia and the terms of Arctic access.
