# Modern realism and Russian lawfare — annotated literature map

**Purpose.** A working bibliography for a PhD on Russian lawfare, organised so that
the theory (Part 1–2) and the case literature (Part 4) meet at an identifiable
research problem (Part 5) rather than sitting in separate chapters that never talk.

**Scope.** All four lawfare arenas, Russia-centred: institutional/UN, maritime and
territorial, armed conflict and international criminal law, and economic/grey-zone
legal coercion.

**See also `docs/06-thesis-design.md`**, which takes the stated thesis design
(definition → case identification → structure → doctrine indicators → opportunism vs.
something deeper) and works out which of the theories below can actually carry it,
where the design is exposed, and who has already published on the same ground.

## 1. Modern realism: what is actually live

Realism is not one theory, and the PhD-relevant question is not "is realism right"
but "which realist variant generates a *testable* claim about legal behaviour".
Most realist writing treats international law as epiphenomenal, which is precisely
why a lawfare thesis cannot simply adopt a realist frame — it has to pick the
variants that have something to say about legal *argument* as a form of power.

### 1.1 Structural realism, and the Ukraine controversy

- Mearsheimer, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* (Norton, 2001; updated 2014). `[u]`
  Offensive realism: great powers maximise relative power; institutions and law are
  "largely a reflection of the distribution of power".
- Mearsheimer, "The False Promise of International Institutions", *International
  Security* 19:3 (1994/95). `[u]`
  **The load-bearing text for this thesis, negatively.** It is the clearest
  statement of the position a lawfare argument must defeat or absorb. If
  institutions merely reflect power, sustained investment in legal argumentation is
  irrational — yet Russia invests heavily. That puzzle is a thesis.
- Mearsheimer, "Why the Ukraine Crisis Is the West's Fault", *Foreign Affairs* 93:5
  (2014); *The Great Delusion* (Yale UP, 2018). `[u]`
- **Edinger, "Offensive ideas: structural realism, classical realism and Putin's war
  on Ukraine", *International Affairs* 98:6 (2022), 1873–. `[v]`**
  The best single entry point to the post-2022 realism dispute. Argues Mearsheimer's
  reading is one realist account among several and does not follow cleanly from his
  own offensive-realist logic. Use this rather than the polemics: it lets you take
  realism seriously without inheriting the Ukraine-blame baggage.
- Further critique: *Analyse & Kritik* 44:2 (2022) carries "Mearsheimer, Realism, and
  the Ukraine War" and "Realism after Ukraine: A Critique of Geopolitical Reason from
  Monroe to Mearsheimer". `[v]` (titles and venue confirmed; authors unverified)

**Purchase for lawfare:** low on its own. Structural realism predicts *that* Russia
will violate constraints, not *how* it will argue. Its value is as the null
hypothesis: legal behaviour is decoration.

### 1.2 Neoclassical realism — the workhorse

- Rose, "Neoclassical Realism and Theories of Foreign Policy", *World Politics* 51:1
  (1998). `[v]` (coined the term, reviewing Brown, Christensen, Schweller, Wohlforth)
- **Ripsman, Taliaferro & Lobell, *Neoclassical Realist Theory of International
  Politics* (OUP, 2016). `[v]`**
  The consolidated statement: systemic pressure is filtered through unit-level
  intervening variables (leader images, strategic culture, state–society relations,
  domestic institutions), with explicit claims about which variables dominate over
  which time horizons.
- Application to Russia: Götz on sphere-of-influence and status motives in the near
  abroad; Krickovic on perception, domestic insecurity and status anxiety; Tsygankov,
  *Russia's Foreign Policy: Change and Continuity in National Identity* (Rowman &
  Littlefield, multiple eds). `[u]`

- **The standing critique you must answer: Legro & Moravcsik, "Is Anybody Still a
  Realist?", *International Security* 24:2 (1999), 5–55. `[v]`**
  Argues that defensive and neoclassical realists rescue realism from anomalies by
  recasting it in forms "theoretically less determinate, less coherent, and less
  distinctive to realism", naming Rose's neoclassical realists directly. Any thesis
  resting on NCR has to show the framework can be disconfirmed. See
  `docs/06-thesis-design.md` §4.

**Purchase for lawfare: high, and under-exploited.** Neoclassical realism has a
ready-made slot for legal argumentation — as an *intervening variable* on the
output side: elite legal culture, the MFA's Legal Department, the codified doctrine
of "legal sovereignty". This is where Mälksoo's material (§4.0) can be wired into a
realist causal chain instead of sitting in a separate "Russian legal thought"
chapter. **A defensible thesis contribution: specify legal-argumentative capacity as
a neoclassical realist intervening variable and test it.**

### 1.3 Status, recognition, honour

- **Paul, Larson & Wohlforth (eds), *Status in World Politics* (CUP, 2014). `[v]`**
  Includes a dedicated treatment of status concerns in Russian foreign policy.
- Tsygankov, *Russia and the West from Alexander to Putin: Honor in International
  Relations* (CUP, 2012). `[v]`

**Purchase for lawfare: high.** Status theory explains something instrumentalism
cannot: why Russia argues at length in forums it expects to lose, and why it
contests *procedure* (standing, jurisdiction, competence) as fiercely as outcomes.
A P5 seat and the Charter's great-power architecture are status goods. Vetoing is
status performance as much as policy blocking.

### 1.4 Hierarchy and order

- Lake, *Hierarchy in International Relations* (Cornell UP, 2009); Bially Mattern &
  Zarakol, "Hierarchies in World Politics", *International Organization* 70:3 (2016). `[u]`
- Cooley & Nexon, *Exit from Hegemony* (OUP, 2020); Cooley, "Countering Democratic
  Norms", *Journal of Democracy* 26:3 (2015). `[u]`
- **Schweller & Pu, "After Unipolarity: China's Visions of International Order in an
  Era of U.S. Decline", *International Security* 36:1 (2011), 41–72. `[v]`**
  Written about China but the mechanism travels: a rising or revisionist power must
  **delegitimate** the incumbent order before balancing against it becomes
  thinkable, using "discursive and cost-imposing practices of resistance". This is
  as close as mainstream realism gets to a theory of lawfare. Read alongside §1.5.

### 1.5 Legitimation realism — the bridge you actually need

- **Goddard, *When Right Makes Might: Rising Powers and World Order* (Cornell UP,
  2018). `[v]`**
  Legitimation strategies — how a rising power justifies its aims in terms of
  existing norms and rules — shape how others respond. Rhetoric is causal, not
  cheap talk, because it constrains the speaker and structures coalitions.
  **The single most useful theoretical import for a lawfare thesis**: it makes legal
  argument a strategic variable with observable consequences, without leaving a
  power-political ontology.
- Norm contestation and resistance: **Bloomfield & Scott (eds), *Norm Antipreneurs and
  the Politics of Resistance to Global Normative Change* (Routledge, 2017)** `[v]`, plus
  Bloomfield, "Norm antipreneurs and theorising resistance to normative change",
  *Review of International Studies* `[v]`; Wiener, *A Theory of Contestation* (Springer,
  2014); Deitelhoff & Zimmermann on norm robustness and erosion. `[u]`
- Applied: "Authoritarian Norm Contestation and International Broadcasting: RT's norm
  contestation strategy during the Ukraine Crisis", *International Politics* (2023). `[v]`

### 1.5a Theories whose subject is behavioural coherence

Realism explains policy *choices*. If the question is whether a pattern of conduct is
strategic, doctrinal, routine or improvised, these are the literatures that take that
as their dependent variable. Argued through in `docs/06-thesis-design.md` §5.

- **Johnston, *Cultural Realism: Strategic Culture and Grand Strategy in Ming China*
  (Princeton UP, 1995), and "Thinking about Strategic Culture", *International
  Security* 19:4 (1995), 32–64. `[v]`**
  Behaviour as the expression of durable, transmitted strategic preferences. Johnston
  sets out three generations of strategic-culture theory and shows the first to be
  unfalsifiable — **use the third generation**, or you import the same defect NCR has.
- **Allison & Zelikow, *Essence of Decision*. `[u]`**
  Organisational process and bureaucratic politics: patterned output with no strategy
  behind it. The strongest rival hypothesis to any "Russian lawfare strategy" claim,
  because a legal department produces legal arguments as its standing function.
- **Adler & Pouliot (eds), *International Practices* (CUP, 2011); Pouliot, "The Logic
  of Practicality", *International Organization* (2008); Pouliot & Cornut, "Practice
  theory and the study of diplomacy", *Cooperation and Conflict* (2015). `[v]`**
  Competent, patterned, recognisable conduct arising from background knowledge and a
  shared repertoire *without* central direction. **The theory that dissolves the
  strategy/opportunism binary instead of picking a side**, and the most likely correct
  answer for the Russian case.

### 1.6 Realism's own theory of international law

A realist lawfare thesis needs this genealogy, because the claim "law is an
instrument of power" is a *legal-theoretical* claim with its own literature, and
citing Mearsheimer for it is a category error.

- Morgenthau, "Positivism, Functionalism, and International Law", *AJIL* 34:2 (1940);
  Carr, *The Twenty Years' Crisis* (1939). `[u]`
  Morgenthau was an international lawyer first. His functionalist critique of legal
  positivism is the origin of the realist account of law — and it is far more
  sophisticated than later realist dismissals.
- Krasner, *Sovereignty: Organized Hypocrisy* (Princeton UP, 1999). `[u]`
  Rules are systematically violated *and* systematically invoked. The gap is the norm.
- **Goldsmith & Posner, *The Limits of International Law* (OUP, 2005) `[v]`**, with
  their retrospective "The Limits of International Law Fifteen Years Later",
  *Chicago Journal of International Law* `[v]`. Rational-choice, not realist proper;
  the cleanest statement of thin instrumentalism.
- **Koskenniemi, "International law and hegemony: a reconfiguration", *Cambridge
  Review of International Affairs* 17:2 (2004). `[v]`**
  International law as "an aspect of hegemonic contestation, a technique of
  articulating political claims in terms of legal rights and duties" — with a
  utopian residue that constrains the arguer. Also *From Apology to Utopia* (CUP,
  2005) and "The Politics of International Law", *EJIL* 1:1 (1990). `[u]`
  **This is the theory of lawfare that the lawfare literature mostly fails to cite.**
- **Hurd, *How to Do Things with International Law* (Princeton UP, 2017). `[v]`**
  Law as permissive and empowering, not merely restraining: compliance and
  legitimation are strategies, and legality is a resource states compete to control.
- **Simpson, *Great Powers and Outlaw States: Unequal Sovereigns in the International
  Legal Order* (CUP, 2004). `[v]`**
  "Legalised hegemony": the Charter order formally encodes great-power privilege
  alongside sovereign equality, with a chapter on San Francisco 1945. Essential for
  any argument about the veto — it reframes Russian P5 conduct as *use of a
  constitutional privilege*, not deviation from the order.
- Also: Byers, *Custom, Power and the Power of Rules* (CUP, 1999); Krisch, "The Decay
  of Consent", *AJIL* 108:1 (2014); Brunnée & Toope, *Legitimacy and Legality in
  International Law* (CUP, 2010) as the interactional counter-position. `[u]`

## 2. Lawfare: the concept and its defects

### 2.1 Origin and definitional trouble

- **Dunlap, "Law and Military Interventions: Preserving Humanitarian Values in 21st
  Century Conflicts" (Nov 2001)**, written after Kosovo; later "Lawfare Today … and
  Tomorrow", *International Law Studies* (US Naval War College). `[v]`
  Lawfare as "the use of law as a weapon of war" / a strategy of using or misusing
  law as a substitute for traditional military means. Note the origin: a US military
  lawyer's worry that adversaries would exploit US compliance.
- **Kittrie, *Lawfare: Law as a Weapon of War* (OUP, 2016). `[v]`**
  The broadest taxonomy — instrumental, compliance-leverage, and "lawfare" as
  litigation strategy. Its opening chapter on meaning, variety and power is the
  standard conceptual survey.
- Kennedy, *Of War and Law* (Princeton UP, 2006). `[u]`
  Law as the vocabulary in which modern war is planned, fought and legitimated —
  a deeper account than the weapon metaphor allows.
- Werner, "The Curious Career of Lawfare", *Case Western Reserve Journal of
  International Law* 43 (2010). `[u]`
  **Read this before using the word.** The term's career is asymmetric: it is
  overwhelmingly applied to *adversaries'* legal activity, so it smuggles in a
  normative judgment. A thesis that uses "lawfare" as an analytic category without
  confronting this will be attacked on it in the viva.

### 2.1a MALOPs — the rival concept, and a completed PhD on adjacent ground

- **Fisher, "Malign Legal Operations (MALOPs) in International Law in the Context of
  Russia's Invasion of Ukraine", PhD dissertation, National University of
  Kyiv-Mohyla Academy (2023) `[v]`**, with "The Kremlin's Malign Legal Operations on
  the Black Sea", *Kyiv-Mohyla Law and Politics Journal* `[v]`; "Russia's Invasion of
  Ukraine and the Doctrine of Malign Legal Operations", *Access to Justice in Eastern
  Europe* `[v]`; "The Origins of 'Lawfare' and the Exploitation of Public
  International Law" `[v]`.
  Argues "lawfare" is inadequate for scholarly and policy use and should be replaced
  by MALOPs — "the exploitation of legal systems by employing disinformation to shape
  perceptions of legitimacy, justify violations, escape legal obligations, contain
  adversaries, or to advantageously revise the rule of law" — and that MALOPs are the
  root of contemporary hybrid warfare. **Directly negates the claim that lawfare is a
  usable discriminating category; positioning against it is unavoidable.** See
  `docs/06-thesis-design.md` §7.
- Policy-facing treatments in the same register: *Journal on Baltic Security*,
  "Russian Lawfare — Russia's Weaponisation of International and Domestic Law"; Just
  Security, "Countering Russian Lawfare and Gray Zone Operations". `[v]`

**Methodological warning.** "Lawfare" is an essentially contested, polemical term
with a military-policy provenance and, more recently, a domestic-politics usage
(prosecutions as "lawfare") that has nothing to do with the IR sense. Either
stipulate a narrow definition and defend it, or drop the label and use
"instrumentalisation of international legal argument", which is what the careful
literature (§1.6) actually studies.

### 2.2 Hybrid, grey-zone and legal resilience

- **Sari, "Legal resilience in an era of grey zone conflicts and hybrid threats",
  *Cambridge Review of International Affairs* 33:6 (2020). `[v]`**
  The sharpest conceptual work in this space. Argues instrumentalisation of law is an
  *integral feature of the international system*, that lawfare/hybrid/grey-zone all
  capture part of it and none offers a complete analytical frame, and proposes
  "legal resilience" as the response concept. Also his EJIL:Talk! piece "Metaphors,
  Rules and War: Making Sense of Hybrid Threats and Grey Zone Conflict". `[v]`
- **Regan & Sari (eds), *Hybrid Threats and Grey Zone Conflict: The Challenge to
  Liberal Democracies* (OUP). `[v]`**
- **Mosquera & Bachmann, "Lawfare in Hybrid Wars: The 21st Century Warfare", *Journal
  of International Humanitarian Legal Studies* 7:1 (2016), 63–87 `[v]`**, and Bachmann
  & Mosquera, "Lawfare and hybrid warfare — how Russia is using the law as a weapon",
  *Amicus Curiae* `[v]`.
  Directly on Russia: annexation via engineered referenda, domestic legal change
  projected onto foreign territory, and the argument for "counter-lawfare". Useful as
  a case inventory; **treat its theory with caution** — it is policy-facing, leans on
  the contested "Gerasimov doctrine" framing, and does not engage §1.6 at all.

## 3. Authoritarian international law — the comparative frame

- **Ginsburg, "Authoritarian International Law?", *AJIL* 114:2 (2020), 221–260. `[v]`**
  Autocracies do not merely evade international law; they *produce* it — norms
  facilitating internal repression, control of online expression, and dilution of
  democracy promotion, through regional organisations and treaty bodies.
- The **AJIL Unbound symposium** in response `[v]`, including "Authoritarian
  International Law, the Use of Force, and Intervention", "The Limits of
  Authoritarian International Law", "Is Authoritarian International Law
  Inevitable?", and "Article 2(4) and Authoritarian International Law".
  **The symposium is more useful than the lead article for a Russia thesis**, because
  the critiques are exactly about whether the pattern is a coherent project or
  parallel opportunism — the same alignment-vs-coordination problem this repo
  attacks quantitatively (§5).
- Domestic-law analogues, for the mechanism by which law serves illiberal ends:
  **Scheppele, "Autocratic Legalism", *University of Chicago Law Review* 85:2 (2018),
  545 `[v]`**; **Ginsburg & Moustafa (eds), *Rule by Law: The Politics of Courts in
  Authoritarian Regimes* (CUP, 2008) `[v]`**; Popova, *Politicized Justice in Emerging
  Democracies* (CUP, 2012) and Trochev on Russian courts. `[u]`
- Comparative: Congyan Cai, *The Rise of China and International Law* (OUP, 2019) `[u]`
  — the obvious control case, and the bridge to this repo's Russia–China data.

## 4. Russia: the four arenas

### 4.0 Foundation — Russian legal thought as an object of study

- **Mälksoo, *Russian Approaches to International Law* (OUP, 2015). `[v]`**
  Uses comparative international law to reconstruct post-Soviet Russian state and
  scholarly approaches, arguing that an idea of Russia's civilisational distinctness
  from the West underpins them. Honourable Mention, 2016 ASEEES Marshall D. Shulman
  Book Prize.
- **Mälksoo, *Russia, the Soviet Union, and Imperial Continuity in International Law*
  (OUP, 2025; ISBN 9780198955931). `[v]`**
  The sequel, tracing imperial ideas and practices through Russian, Soviet and
  post-1991 engagement with international law. **The most current monograph available;
  build the doctrinal chapter on this.**
- **Mälksoo, "The Annexation of Crimea and Balance of Power in International Law",
  *EJIL* 30:1 (2019), 303–319. `[v]`**
  Review essay; rejects the suggestion that international law should accommodate a
  return to balance-of-power politics. **Directly addresses the realism/law interface
  — cite it where your theory chapter meets your legal chapter.**
- Bowring, *Law, Rights and Ideology in Russia* (Routledge, 2013). `[u]`

### 4.0a The three Allison articles — read these first

Roy Allison (Oxford) has produced the closest thing to an existing
realism-meets-lawfare research programme on Russia, and your thesis has to position
against it.

- **Allison, "Russian 'deniable' intervention in Ukraine: how and why Russia broke the
  rules", *International Affairs* 90:6 (2014), 1255–1297. `[v]`**
  Denial, plausible deniability and contestable legal/political claims as
  instruments that *disabled international responses*. Legal argument as operational
  cover.
- **Allison, "Russia and the post-2014 international legal order: revisionism and
  realpolitik", *International Affairs* 93:3 (2017), 519–543. `[v]`**
- **Allison, "Russia's Case for War against Ukraine: Legal Claims, Political Rhetoric,
  and Instrumentality in a Fracturing International Order", *Problems of
  Post-Communism* 71:3 (2023/24). `[v]`**
  Qualitative content analysis of Russian speeches and texts on the war: rhetorical
  character, intended audiences, and the instrumentality of the irredentist claims on
  "historic Russian regions". The post-2022 extension of the programme below.
- **Allison, "Russian Revisionism, Legal Discourse and the 'Rules-Based' International
  Order", *Europe-Asia Studies* 72:6 (2020), 976–995. `[v]`**
  **This is the finding your thesis must either extend or overturn.** Examining
  Russian legal discourse 2014–2019 through the Ukraine and Syria interventions, it
  finds **little evidence of sustained legal revisionism** — i.e. of an effort to
  *modify* international law — while confirming that Russia deploys legal rhetoric
  strategically and instrumentally, and asserts legal exceptionalism in the CIS
  space.
  Two live openings: (a) the period is pre-2022, and 2022–2026 may show a shift from
  instrumental rhetoric to constructive counter-doctrine; (b) it studies *discourse*,
  so a design measuring *institutional and procedural* behaviour (§5) could reach a
  different answer without contradicting it.

### 4.1 Arena I — the UN, the Charter and institutional lawfare

- **Simpson (§1.6) is the theoretical anchor**: the veto is a constitutional
  privilege of legalised hegemony, not an anomaly.
- The abusive-veto debate:
  - "The war in Ukraine and legal limitations on Russian vetoes", *Journal on the Use
    of Force and International Law* (2023). `[v]` (author unverified)
  - "Locating the Veto Power in the International Legal Order: When a Permanent
    Member of the UN Security Council Becomes an Aggressor" (Springer chapter). `[v]`
  - **Security Council Report, *The Veto* research report (March 2026) and its
    working-methods pages. `[v]` Primary-adjacent, current, and the best source on
    penholdership, blocked drafts and the veto-initiative practice.**
  - Note for the argument: a claim that a veto shielding one's own manifest
    aggression is *legally void* is itself a lawfare move by the other side. Symmetry
    is analytically important and rhetorically dangerous — handle explicitly.
- The GA route: the Ukraine emergency special sessions (ES-11), the 2022 veto
  initiative (A/RES/76/262), and suspension from the Human Rights Council. These are
  contests over *competence*, which is the institutional-lawfare core.
- **This repo's data speaks to this arena directly.** `docs/03-findings-2026-09.md`
  §1 quantifies Russian and Chinese positional behaviour across GA and Council
  votes, including `withheld_from_majority` (distance-from-majority) and the
  finding that Russia takes the more negative position in 95 of 101 contested GA
  splits in 2021–2026. See §6.

### 4.2 Arena II — maritime and territorial legal contestation

**Crimea and the Sea of Azov**

- Marxsen, "The Crimea Crisis: An International Law Perspective", *ZaöRV* 74 (2014);
  Grant, *Aggression against Ukraine* (Palgrave, 2015); Sayapin & Tsybulenko (eds),
  *The Use of Force against Ukraine and International Law* (Springer, 2018). `[u]`
- Kerch Strait: the ITLOS provisional-measures order on the *Detention of Three
  Ukrainian Naval Vessels* (2019) and the UNCLOS Annex VII arbitration on *Coastal
  State Rights in the Black Sea, Sea of Azov and Kerch Strait* — including Russia's
  Article 298 declaration and its jurisdictional strategy. `[u]` (verify case details)
- Investment arbitration as a territorial-status back door — an underused and very
  rich seam:
  - **Everest Estate LLC v. Russia**, jurisdictional decision 20 March 2017: the
    1998 Russia–Ukraine BIT applied to Crimean investments made before 2014,
    reasoning that an investment need not have been in the respondent's territory
    when made. Hague Court of Appeal dismissed set-aside applications, holding no
    finding on Crimean sovereignty was required for jurisdiction. `[v]`
  - "The Applicability of the Russia–Ukraine Bilateral Investment Treaty to Crimea in
    the Light of the Duty of Non-recognition in International Law", *Journal of
    International Arbitration* 38:5 (2021) `[v]`; "Arbitral Jurisdiction in Crimea
    Investment Disputes: Connecting the Dots", *Goettingen Journal of International
    Law* `[v]`.
  - **Why it matters theoretically:** tribunals reached Russia's liability by
    *avoiding* the sovereignty question. Russia's own treaty, invoked against it,
    became a lever — a clean case of lawfare's reversibility, and evidence against
    pure instrumentalism (you cannot control the instrument once you have built it).

**Arctic and the Northern Sea Route**

- **Solski (Norwegian Centre for the Law of the Sea, UiT), "The Genesis of Article 234
  of the UNCLOS", *Ocean Development & International Law* (2020) `[v]`**, plus "The
  'Due Regard' of Article 234", *ODIL* (2021); "Northern Sea Route Permit Scheme:
  Does Article 234 of UNCLOS Allow Prior Authorization?", *Ocean Yearbook* (2021);
  "The Northern Sea Route at the crossroads: what lies ahead after the war in
  Ukraine?", *The Polar Journal* (2022). `[v]`
  Article 234 is the key text: an exceptional ice-covered-areas provision Russia
  uses to ground NSR navigation rules, opposed by the United States. **The textbook
  example of maximalist good-faith interpretation of a genuinely ambiguous
  provision — lawfare that never leaves the four corners of the treaty.**
- Also: the *ODIL*/*Polar Journal* special issue "Regulating shipping in Russian
  Arctic waters: between international law, national interests and geopolitics"
  `[v]`; Gavrilov on the environmental dimension of Article 234 (a Russian-authored
  position, i.e. a primary source on the Russian legal argument) `[v]`; "The Polar
  Silk Road and the future governance of the Northern Sea Route", *Leiden Journal of
  International Law* `[v]`; Byers, *International Law and the Arctic* (CUP, 2013). `[u]`
- CLCS: Russia's Article 76 extended-continental-shelf submissions (Lomonosov Ridge)
  are the counter-case — **rule-following, queue-joining behaviour** in the same
  region and period as NSR maximalism. Any thesis claiming a coherent Russian lawfare
  strategy has to explain this variance. It may be the best within-country
  comparison available.

### 4.3 Arena III — armed conflict, ICJ/ICC and counter-lawfare

- **ICJ, *Application of the ICSFT and of CERD (Ukraine v. Russian Federation)*,
  judgment 31 January 2024. `[v]`** Findings on ICSFT investigative obligations and
  Article 12, and on CERD Articles 2(1)(a) and 5(e)(v) regarding the education system
  in Crimea after 2014.
- **ICJ, *Allegations of Genocide (Ukraine v. Russian Federation)*, preliminary
  objections, 2 February 2024. `[v]`**
- Commentary: EJIL:Talk!, "Unfulfilled Promises of the ICJ Litigation for Ukraine",
  and "ICJ Delivers Preliminary Objections Judgment … Ukraine Loses on the Most
  Important Aspects"; the *AJIL* case note on the ICSFT/CERD judgment; FIDH's
  "mixed decisions" assessment. `[v]`
  **These are the most important texts for the thesis's hardest question: what
  happens when counter-lawfare largely fails.** Ukraine's compromissory-clause
  strategy (suing on terrorism-financing and racial-discrimination treaties because
  aggression is unreachable) produced thin wins. That is a finding about the *limits*
  of legal instruments for the weaker party.
- **Kuc (Warsaw), "The Empire Strikes Back: Russia's Procedural Offensive Before
  International Courts and Tribunals", EJIL:Talk! `[v]`**
  Procedural lawfare as such: jurisdictional objections, non-appearance, delay,
  counter-claims. **The closest existing statement of the thesis-shaped claim that
  Russian lawfare is primarily procedural rather than doctrinal.**
- **Marchuk (Copenhagen). `[v]`** Publishes on Ukraine's pursuit of accountability
  across fora, powerful states' approaches to international law, and international
  strategic litigation arising from armed conflict; has advised the Ukrainian
  government on strategic litigation and ICC communications. PI of the 3-year
  **EASTERNISATION** project (Danish Research Fund) tracing **evolving Russian and
  Chinese narratives of international law** — *directly overlapping your topic and
  this repo's dyad; check its outputs and consider contact.*
- **Special Tribunal for the Crime of Aggression against Ukraine `[v]`**: requested by
  Ukraine 13 May 2025, endorsed by CoE foreign ministers the following day, agreement
  signed in Strasbourg 25 June 2025, sited within the Council of Europe via an
  enlarged partial agreement, jurisdiction resting on Ukraine's territorial
  jurisdiction and confined to aggression. Amnesty's intervention on victim-centred
  justice marks the contested points. **Institution-building as counter-lawfare, in
  real time — a natural final substantive chapter.**
- Russia and the ECHR — legal exit as strategy:
  - 2020 constitutional amendment to **Article 79** (decisions of interstate bodies
    not enforceable if contrary to the Constitution); Venice Commission
    recommended removal or amendment. `[v]`
  - Russia left the Council of Europe **16 March 2022**; ceased to be a party to the
    ECHR **16 September 2022** under the Article 58 six-month notice. `[v]`
  - Verfassungsblog, "A Backdoor Exit from the European Convention on Human Rights:
    Russia, the Council of Europe and Article 58(3) ECHR"; ICLQ, "Council of Europe
    Expulsion and the European Convention on Human Rights: The Foundations of
    Involuntary Treaty Withdrawal"; ECtHR Resolution of 22 March 2022 on cessation
    of membership. `[v]`
  - **Mälksoo & Benedek (eds), *Russia and the European Court of Human Rights: The
    Strasbourg Effect* (CUP, 2017/2018). `[v]`** The essential *before* picture:
    reception of Convention standards in a decaying-democratic and authoritarian
    setting. Read with the *ICON* review symposium. `[v]`
  - **Why this arena is the best test case in the thesis:** Russia spent two decades
    as the Court's heaviest-litigated respondent, then built a constitutional
    override, then exited. That sequence — engage, hollow, exit — is a *strategy*,
    observable, dated, and hard to explain as either compliance or simple defection.

### 4.4 Arena IV — economic and grey-zone legal coercion

- **Farrell & Newman, "Weaponized Interdependence: How Global Economic Networks Shape
  State Coercion", *International Security* 44:1 (2019), 42–79. `[v]`**
  Panopticon and chokepoint effects; states with authority over central network nodes
  can coerce. The structural theory of why *Western* legal-economic instruments bite
  and why Russia's response is jurisdictional.
- **Mulder, *The Economic Weapon: The Rise of Sanctions as a Tool of Modern War* (Yale
  UP, 2022). `[v]`**
  The historical corrective: sanctions as a liberal-internationalist legal
  instrument from the League onward, which *backfired against larger authoritarian
  states* by encouraging autarky. Directly relevant to post-2022 Russia.
- Drezner, *The Sanctions Paradox* (CUP, 1999); Ruys & Ryngaert on unilateral
  sanctions and extraterritoriality. `[u]`
- **Russian counter-lawfare through jurisdiction — the "Lugovoy law". `[v]`**
  Articles 248.1 and 248.2 of the Arbitrazh Procedure Code, introduced by Federal Law
  171-FZ of 8 June 2020, give Russian commercial courts exclusive jurisdiction over
  disputes involving sanctioned persons and allow anti-suit injunctions overriding
  valid arbitration and forum clauses. Practice has expanded well beyond the original
  design: the threshold shifted from proving impeded access to justice to the mere
  existence of sanctions, and on **28 November 2024** the Supreme Court held that
  Russian courts may take exclusive jurisdiction even over disputes between two
  Russian-domiciled companies, overriding their arbitration agreement. The
  Constitutional Court declined a challenge to the provisions.
  Sources are practitioner-side (Kluwer Arbitration Blog, CIS Arbitration Forum,
  Transnational Litigation Blog, Jus Mundi) `[v]` — **a genuine academic gap, and
  therefore an opportunity: there is no strong IR-theoretic treatment of
  jurisdictional counter-sanctions as statecraft.**
- Domestic law projected outward: "foreign agent" and "undesirable organisation"
  legislation, and transnational repression. Connect to §3 (autocratic legalism) and
  to Ginsburg's claim that autocracies export permissive norms. `[u]` — verify the
  current literature; it has moved fast since 2022.

## 5. The research problem worth building a thesis on

This repo exists because of a specific inference failure: **alignment is not
coordination** (`docs/01-methodology.md`). The lawfare literature has the identical
failure in legal form, and nobody has named it.

> **The lawfare inference problem.** Observing that a state advanced a legal argument
> that served its interests does not establish that the argument was *instrumental*.
> The observation is equally consistent with (a) strategic instrumentalisation,
> (b) sincere but self-serving doctrine — legal culture genuinely held, as Mälksoo
> documents, (c) bureaucratic routine: the MFA Legal Department produces legal
> arguments because that is its function, and (d) *convergence* — the argument is
> simply available and any state in that position would make it.

Nearly every "Russian lawfare" claim in the policy literature runs on the weakest of
these and reads as though it had established the strongest. Allison 2020 is the main
exception, and it is careful precisely because it looks for the *modification* of law
rather than its use.

**What this yields, concretely.**

1. **An evidence hierarchy for lawfare claims**, on the model of the one in this
   repo's README. Strongest to weakest, as a first draft:
   1. codified doctrinal change — a new treaty text, reservation, declaration or
      official legal position Russia authored and others adopted;
   2. institutional construction — a forum, tribunal or procedure built to host a
      Russian legal position;
   3. procedural conduct in litigation — objections, non-appearance, counter-claims
      (Kuc's territory), which is costly and therefore informative;
   4. legislative projection — domestic law with extraterritorial reach (§4.4);
   5. sustained formulation adoption with measurable lead–lag (this repo's M6);
   6. official legal rhetoric in statements, EOVs and explanatory memoranda;
   7. commentary asserting that a state "used law as a weapon".
   Tiers 6–7 are where the field lives. **Building and then applying this hierarchy
   is a defensible original contribution in itself.**

2. **Base rates for legal argument.** This is the direct transplant of measure M2.
   Before claiming that a Russian legal manoeuvre is distinctively instrumental,
   compute the same measure for states in comparable positions: the US on Nicaragua
   jurisdiction and the ICC, China on the South China Sea award, India on Article 76,
   Canada on Article 234 (Canada, not only Russia, grounds Arctic shipping rules in
   it). If the "Russian" pattern is modal great-power behaviour, the finding inverts —
   exactly as this repo's Russia–China voting result inverted against the Global
   South mean.

3. **Within-Russia variance as the identifying strategy.** The strongest designs
   exploit cases where Russia behaved *differently*:
   - NSR maximalism vs. CLCS queue-joining, same region, same decade (§4.2);
   - twenty years of ECtHR engagement, then override, then exit (§4.3);
   - defending Crimea BIT claims on the merits while refusing ICJ jurisdiction;
   - vetoing while China abstains — partial alignment, and evidence about how much of
     the legal position is jointly held (§6).
   Variance within one state controls for legal culture and for regime type, which
   cross-national designs cannot.

**Candidate questions, each survivable:**

- *Is Russian lawfare doctrinal or procedural?* Tests Allison 2020's null (no
  sustained revisionism) against Kuc's procedural-offensive claim. Answerable from
  court and tribunal records.
- *Does legal-argumentative capacity function as a neoclassical realist intervening
  variable?* (§1.2) Requires specifying the variable and finding variance in it.
- *Does Russia build legal institutions, or only block them?* The distinction between
  Ginsburg's authoritarian *production* of international law and mere obstruction —
  and the sharpest available test of whether "revisionism" is the right word.
- *Why does Russia argue in forums it expects to lose?* Status theory (§1.3) vs.
  instrumentalism (§1.6) vs. audience-directed legitimation (Goddard, §1.5).

## 6. Where this repo's data attaches

`rcwatch` was built for the Russia–China coordination question, but three of its
components are reusable for the lawfare project without modification:

- **M6, formulation tracking** (`src/rcwatch/phrases.py`, dictionary in
  `docs/02-source-inventory.md` §5). A marker-phrase dictionary with lead–lag
  measurement is exactly the instrument needed for tier 5 of the hierarchy above.
  Extend the dictionary with legal formulations: "indivisible security", "Charter-based
  international order" versus "rules-based order", "unilateral coercive measures",
  "legitimate security interests", "special military operation" as a legal
  characterisation.
- **M3b, distance from the majority** (`agreement.withheld_from_majority`). Positional
  extremity on legal-institutional texts — competence, procedure, mandate renewals —
  is a better measure of institutional lawfare than dyadic agreement is.
- **The provenance and verification discipline** (`data/seed/*.csv`,
  `verification` status, `make audit`). Apply it unchanged to a table of legal
  positions: one row per official Russian legal argument, with forum, date, source
  URL, evidence tier, and verification status. **That table is the empirical spine of
  the thesis, and it is the same schema pattern already in `data/schema/`.**

The Russia–China dyad is also a live comparative asset. Marchuk's EASTERNISATION
project is tracing Russian *and Chinese* narratives of international law, and this
repo already measures where the two governments' positions coincide and diverge.

## 7. Traps

1. **Using "lawfare" unreflectively.** Werner's critique (§2.1) is fatal if
   unaddressed: the term is applied asymmetrically and carries a judgment.
2. **Importing Mearsheimer's Ukraine position along with his theory.** §1.1 —
   Edinger lets you separate them.
3. **Citing Mearsheimer for the proposition that law is an instrument of power.**
   Category error; the claim belongs to Morgenthau, Koskenniemi, Krasner and Hurd.
4. **Treating Russian legal argument as insincere by assumption.** Mälksoo's work
   makes sincerity the more interesting hypothesis, and the harder one to dismiss.
5. **No base rate.** The single most common defect in the policy literature, and the
   one this repo exists to correct in the voting domain (§5.2).
6. **Mirror-imaging.** Ukraine's litigation strategy, the aggression tribunal and the
   abusive-veto argument are also instrumental uses of law. Symmetry of *description*
   is not equivalence of *justification* — say so explicitly rather than avoiding the
   observation.
7. **State media as secondary sources.** Already covered in
   `docs/01-methodology.md`: TASS and RT are legitimate primary evidence of what a
   government wants said, and unreliable evidence of what happened.
