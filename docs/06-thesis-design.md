# Thesis design: does the lawfare project still work with neoclassical realism?

Companion to `docs/05-realism-lawfare-literature.md`. That doc maps the reading;
this one addresses the design as stated:

> Lawfare is a real academic term that can discriminate. Apply the definition to
> Russian practice → identify the Russian lawfare cases → analyse them → check
> whether they are a structured part of Russian foreign policy → look for
> indicators of doctrine or strategy → is Russia's use opportunistic, or is there
> something deeper behind it?

Short answer on the theory question: **neoclassical realism still works, but not in
the role it looks like it should play, and if you leave it where it is now it will
be attacked as unfalsifiable.** The design needs one structural fix first, because
the theory choice depends on it.

---

## 1. There are two dependent variables here, and they need separating

| | Question | Type |
|---|---|---|
| **DV1** | Is this case lawfare? | Classification. Belongs to the definitional chapter. |
| **DV2** | Is Russian lawfare structured/strategic, or opportunistic? | A property of the *aggregate pattern* across cases. |

The slide between them is where the design is currently vulnerable. DV1 is
conceptual work; DV2 is causal-explanatory work. Neither is a *foreign policy
choice*, which matters for the theory question (§4): almost all of modern realism,
neoclassical realism included, takes policy choices as its dependent variable.

## 2. The selection problem — fix this before anything else

As stated, step 2 selects cases **because** they satisfy the lawfare definition, and
step 3 then asks whether the resulting set is patterned.

A set assembled on the criterion "instrumental legal behaviour" will always look
patterned. The pattern is an artefact of the selection rule, not a finding about
Russian foreign policy. This is selection on the dependent variable and it is the
first thing an examiner will say.

**The fix is not a caveat; it is the proof of your own first claim.**

Your claim 1 is that lawfare *discriminates*. A concept that discriminates must
generate **negative cases** — instances it excludes. If every piece of Russian legal
argumentation you examine comes out as lawfare, the concept has no discriminating
power and claim 1 fails on its own terms. So the negative cases are not a concession
to method; they are the evidence for the thesis's opening move.

**Negative cases worth building in** (Russia using international law in ways your
definition should exclude):

- **The Arctic CLCS submissions.** Russia queued under UNCLOS Article 76, filed
  scientific evidence, and waited on a Commission recommendation — ordinary
  rule-following, in the same region and decade as its maximalist Article 234
  position on the Northern Sea Route. **The single most valuable case in the
  thesis**, because it is a within-Russia, within-region, within-period contrast.
- **Two decades of substantial ECtHR practice.** Before the override and exit,
  Russia was the Court's most-litigated respondent and paid a great many ordinary
  judgments. That compliance record is the baseline against which the 2020
  constitutional override and 2022 exit become legible as strategy.
- **WTO dispute settlement participation**, treaty ratifications entered without
  reservation, routine consular and extradition practice.

**Near-miss cases** (legal behaviour that resembles lawfare but is ordinary
advocacy): jurisdictional objections that any respondent state's counsel would file;
treaty interpretations that are contestable but within the ordinary range;
positions Russia shares with states nobody accuses of lawfare — note again that
**Canada**, not only Russia, grounds Arctic shipping regulation in Article 234.

**What this buys you.** Once negative cases are in, restate DV2 as:

> **What explains variation in Russia's resort to, and choice of, legal
> instruments?**

Three things follow. The design stops being descriptive and becomes explanatory.
The strategy/opportunism verdict becomes an *output* of the variance analysis
instead of a question you assert an answer to. And — decisively for §4 —
neoclassical realism becomes genuinely useful, because variation in foreign policy
behaviour under roughly constant structural pressure is precisely what it was built
to explain.

## 3. Indicators of doctrine or strategy

Ordered by **how hard each is to fake**, which is the property that makes an
indicator worth having.

1. **Pre-positioning.** Legal groundwork laid before the operational need exists.
   Candidates: the Article 298 UNCLOS declaration excluding categories of dispute
   from compulsory jurisdiction, years before Kerch; the 2020 Article 79
   constitutional amendment, two years before the ECHR exit; reservations and
   opt-outs filed in advance of the disputes they later govern.
   **This is the best single discriminator you have.** An opportunist cannot
   pre-position — not as a matter of evidence, but definitionally. Every confirmed
   instance of pre-positioning is direct evidence against the opportunism reading.
2. **Organisational investment.** The MFA Legal Department (its director, headcount,
   budget line, and the seniority of legal advisers attached to delegations); the
   International Law Advisory Board under the MFA; the Prosecutor General's General
   Department of International Legal Cooperation; MGIMO's international law
   department and the Russian Association of International Law as state-cultivated
   doctrine producers. Costly, slow-moving, and visible in open sources.
3. **Sequencing across institutions.** February 2022 is the test: recognition of the
   DPR/LPR, then the "friendship and mutual assistance" treaties, then the Article 51
   letter to the Security Council, in a tight ordered chain. Cross-institutional
   sequencing on a short timetable is hard to produce without coordination.
4. **Cross-arena consistency of a specific argument.** Does the sovereignty and
   non-intervention formulation deployed at the Security Council reappear in ECtHR
   pleadings, in Arctic submissions, in the Article 248.1 case law? This is where
   `rcwatch`'s M6 machinery applies directly.
5. **Costly consistency.** Does Russia hold a legal position when it becomes
   expensive? The Kosovo/Crimea self-determination symmetry cut against Russia's own
   Chechnya position and complicated Abkhazia and South Ossetia. Did it pay that
   cost, or quietly drop the argument? Opportunists abandon; strategists pay.
6. **Formulation lead–lag.** Do Russian formulations propagate into third states'
   texts, and how fast? Agenda-setting is not available to an opportunist.
7. **Doctrinal codification.** Weakest — and see the warning below.

**Indicators of opportunism:** justification appearing only after the act; argument
inconsistency across arenas; abandonment of positions once inconvenient; no
identifiable organisational locus; reliance on ad hoc external counsel; a purely
reactive profile that never initiates.

### Expect the codification indicator to come back negative

This is worth knowing in advance, because it will look like a failed chapter when it
is actually a finding.

Russian doctrinal documents present Russia as a **defender** of international law,
not a practitioner of lawfare. The 2023 Foreign Policy Concept treats "ensuring the
rule of law in international relations" as a foundation of world order, states that
Russia advocates strengthening the legal fundamentals of international relations and
complies faithfully with its obligations, and sets Charter-based international law
explicitly *against* the Western "rules-based order" as the latter's informality is
the thing being criticised. The 2014 Military Doctrine mentions legal instruments
only among the non-violent measures to be used before force — a last-resort framing
that appears in most states' doctrine and carries almost no evidentiary weight.
`[verify the 2014 text directly: the egress proxy blocked both copies this pass]`

Three consequences:

- **You will not find a codified Russian lawfare doctrine.** Design the thesis so
  that no chapter depends on finding one.
- The indicators that matter are therefore the ones not built on self-description:
  pre-positioning, organisational investment, sequencing.
- The legalist self-image becomes the interesting object rather than an obstacle.
  If Russian lawfare works *through* a sincere-seeming commitment to formal legality
  against Western informalism, that is a far more interesting mechanism than cynical
  abuse, it is harder to counter, and it is consistent with what Mälksoo documents
  about Russian legal thought. Consider making it the thesis's central claim.

## 4. Neoclassical realism: what it can and cannot do here

**What it does well.** It supplies the *demand* side. Relative decline, perceived
encirclement, status deficit and — after 2022 — conventional military
underperformance all generate demand for low-cost instruments of influence, and
legal instruments are among the cheapest available. NCR's intervening variables
(leader images, strategic culture, state–society relations, domestic institutions)
give you a transmission mechanism from that structural pressure to actual legal
conduct, and they have a natural slot for the thing you care about: **legal-
argumentative capacity as an intervening variable** — elite legal culture, the MFA
Legal Department, the state-funded doctrinal academy. Specifying that variable and
testing it is a real contribution.

**Where it breaks on your question as currently framed.** NCR is symmetrically
compatible with both possible answers to DV2:

- lawfare turns out strategic → the domestic filters transmitted structural pressure
  efficiently;
- lawfare turns out opportunistic → the filters distorted it.

Either finding confirms the framework. A framework compatible with all your possible
results is doing no work, and there is a canonical citation for exactly this charge:
**Legro & Moravcsik, "Is Anybody Still a Realist?", *International Security* 24:2
(1999), 5–55**, which argues that defensive and neoclassical realists rescue realism
from anomalies by recasting it in forms "theoretically less determinate, less
coherent, and less distinctive to realism", and which names Rose's neoclassical
realists directly. Assume your examiner has read it.

**Verdict.** Keep neoclassical realism, but demote it: it is the framework
explaining why lawfare is in demand and how structural pressure reaches legal
conduct. It is not the theory that answers whether the conduct is coherent. Once
DV2 is restated as variance (§2), NCR earns its place, because variance is what it
explains. Left as "is it strategic or opportunistic", it cannot lose, which means it
cannot win either.

## 5. Recommended architecture

Four layers, each doing a distinct job.

**Layer 1 — Framework: neoclassical realism.** Why legal instruments are in demand,
why now, why at this intensity. Transmission via specified intervening variables.

**Layer 2 — The discriminating question: three rival process theories, run against
each other.** These are theories whose actual subject is the coherence of state
behaviour, which is what DV2 asks about.

- **Strategic culture.** Johnston, *Cultural Realism* (Princeton UP, 1995) and
  "Thinking about Strategic Culture", *International Security* 19:4 (1995), 32–64.
  Behaviour follows durable, transmitted preferences. **Use Johnston's third
  generation, not the first** — his own critique is that first-generation strategic
  culture is unfalsifiable, and adopting it would import the same defect NCR has.
- **Organisational process.** Allison & Zelikow, *Essence of Decision*. Patterned
  behaviour with no strategy behind it, because the MFA Legal Department produces
  legal arguments as its standing function. **This is your strongest rival
  hypothesis and it is probably true in part** — treat it as a serious contender,
  not a straw man.
- **Practice theory.** Adler & Pouliot (eds), *International Practices* (CUP, 2011);
  Pouliot, "The Logic of Practicality", *International Organization* (2008); Pouliot
  & Cornut on diplomacy as practice, *Cooperation and Conflict* (2015).
  **My primary recommendation, because it dissolves your binary rather than
  answering it.** Practice theory's core claim is that competent, patterned,
  recognisable behaviour arises from background knowledge and a shared repertoire
  *without* central direction. That makes "structured but not strategic" a third,
  theorised outcome instead of a fudge — and given what you will probably find (no
  codified doctrine, genuine organisational capacity, strong consistency in some
  arenas and none in others), it is the most likely correct answer and the most
  publishable, because it is a theoretical result rather than a verdict on Russian
  intentions.

**Layer 3 — Operational typology: Bloomfield's antipreneur/entrepreneur
distinction.** Bloomfield & Scott (eds), *Norm Antipreneurs* (Routledge, 2017) and
Bloomfield in *Review of International Studies*. Antipreneur = reactive, defensive,
blocking. Entrepreneur = proactive, constructive, norm-building. That maps almost
exactly onto opportunism versus strategy, and it is already in print — so you
inherit a validated coding scheme instead of inventing one and defending it.

**Layer 4 — The alternative to "strategy" you must take seriously: continuity.**
Mälksoo, *Russia, the Soviet Union, and Imperial Continuity in International Law*
(OUP, 2025) argues that imperial legal ideas and practices carry across the 1917 and
1991 regime breaks. If the same legal moves recur across Tsarist, Soviet and
Federation periods, then the "something deeper behind it" is **inheritance rather
than design**. That is a fourth answer to your question, it is not reducible to
either horn of your binary, and it may be the most interesting result available.

**Making it causal:** Goddard, *When Right Makes Might* (Cornell UP, 2018) is what
stops the whole thing being descriptive. Legitimation has consequences — it entraps
the speaker and structures third-party coalitions — so legal argument matters even
when insincere. That is the proposition that makes the thesis matter beyond Russia.

**Demote or drop:** offensive and structural realism (no purchase on legal
argument); Mearsheimer on Ukraine specifically (Edinger, *International Affairs*
98:6 (2022) lets you cite the debate without inheriting the position).

## 6. Rival hypotheses and what would discriminate between them

| Hypothesis | Predicts | Discriminating observation |
|---|---|---|
| **Strategy** — centrally directed, doctrinally anchored | Pre-positioning; cross-arena consistency; costly consistency maintained; identifiable coordinating body | Legal instruments in place before the disputes they serve; the same formulation across unconnected forums |
| **Opportunism** — ad hoc, reactive | Post-hoc justification only; inconsistency across arenas; positions abandoned when costly | No pre-positioning anywhere; arguments appear only after the operational fact |
| **Organisational routine** | Patterned output, no strategic direction; volume tracks bureaucratic capacity not political priority | Legal output continues at constant volume across political shifts; arguments track departmental competence boundaries |
| **Practice / competent improvisation** | Recognisable repertoire, locally improvised; consistency of *form* with variation in *content* | Same moves recur in new situations without doctrinal instruction; practitioners cannot articulate the rule they follow |
| **Imperial continuity** | Recurrence of specific legal arguments across regime breaks | Pre-1917 and Soviet-era analogues of the same argument, independent of current doctrine |

Note that **the last three are all "structured but not strategic"**, and they are
distinguishable from each other. That is the real analytical payoff of the design,
and it is only reachable if the binary is abandoned early.

## 7. Existing work you must position against

### Fisher and MALOPs — a completed PhD on adjacent ground

**Brad Fisher (Major, US Air Force), "Malign Legal Operations (MALOPs) in
International Law in the Context of Russia's Invasion of Ukraine", PhD dissertation,
National University of Kyiv-Mohyla Academy, 2023.** With "The Kremlin's Malign Legal
Operations on the Black Sea: Analyzing the Exploitation of Public International Law
Against Ukraine", *Kyiv-Mohyla Law and Politics Journal*; "Russia's Invasion of
Ukraine and the Doctrine of Malign Legal Operations", *Access to Justice in Eastern
Europe*; and "The Origins of 'Lawfare' and the Exploitation of Public International
Law".

His central move is the **direct negation of your claim 1**: that "lawfare" is
inadequate for scholarly or policy use and should be replaced by MALOPs — "the
exploitation of legal systems by employing disinformation to shape perceptions of
legitimacy, justify violations, escape legal obligations, contain adversaries, or to
advantageously revise the rule of law". He also asserts MALOPs are the root of
contemporary hybrid warfare, and reads Russian MALOPs as near-certain warning
indicators before the 2022 invasion.

**Read this as an opportunity.** You now have a named interlocutor and a live
disagreement, which is worth more than an empty field. And you have the better
argument available to you:

- MALOPs builds its verdict into its definition — *malign*, *exploitation*,
  *disinformation*. A concept that only ever classifies the conduct it disapproves of
  has no negative cases, and a concept with no negative cases cannot discriminate.
  It labels; it does not sort.
- Your claim is precisely that a **neutral** definition sorts better. §2 above is
  how you prove it: build the negative and near-miss cases in, and show your
  definition excluding things MALOPs would have to swallow.
- His disinformation criterion also makes the concept unavailable for exactly the
  cases that matter most — Article 234, the CLCS submissions, the Article 298
  declaration — which involve no disinformation whatever and are arguably the
  purest instances of legal instrumentality in the Russian record.

### Allison — four articles, effectively the same project on the discourse side

Add to the three in `docs/05`:

**Allison, "Russia's Case for War against Ukraine: Legal Claims, Political Rhetoric,
and Instrumentality in a Fracturing International Order", *Problems of
Post-Communism* 71:3 (2023/24).** Qualitative content analysis of Russian speeches
and texts, assessing rhetorical character, intended audiences, and the
instrumentality of the irredentist claims.

With Allison 2014, 2017 and 2020, this is a sustained programme on Russian legal
argumentation, and his 2020 finding — **little evidence of sustained legal
revisionism, but confirmed strategic and instrumental deployment of legal
rhetoric** — is already a partial answer to your DV2. Your distinct contribution has
to be one of: the institutional and procedural record rather than discourse; the
post-2022 period; or the negative cases he does not examine. Probably all three.

## 8. Chapter architecture that follows from this

1. **The concept.** Lawfare's career and its defects (Dunlap, Kittrie, Werner);
   the rival concepts (MALOPs, hybrid warfare, grey zone); your definition, stated
   as necessary and sufficient conditions; **and a demonstration that it excludes
   things** — the discrimination claim, proved on near-miss cases.
2. **Theory.** NCR as framework; the three process theories as rivals; continuity as
   the fourth path; the observable implications of each, fixed in advance.
3. **The case universe.** How cases were identified, *including negative cases*, with
   the evidence hierarchy from `docs/05` §5 and a coding table carrying source and
   verification status per row.
4. **Indicators chapter.** The ladder in §3, applied. Expect the doctrine indicator
   to be negative and make that a finding.
5–7. **Arena chapters.** Institutional/UN; maritime and territorial (with the
   Article 234 / CLCS contrast as the analytical centre); courts and tribunals
   (procedural conduct, the ECtHR sequence, the 2024 ICJ judgments).
8. **Economic-legal and jurisdictional counter-lawfare.** Article 248.1 and the
   sanctions response — the least-studied arena and your clearest claim to novelty.
9. **Synthesis.** Which hypothesis survives; what it implies for the concept; what it
   implies for realism's treatment of international law.
